You've hit on a crucial point—**human error is a major vulnerability**—so let's refine the hierarchy. While all layers matter, the **absolute first line of defense must be physical access control**. Here’s why:

1. **Access Control (Guards, Bag Checks, Keycards)**  
   - **Primary Role**: Prevents unauthorized entry *before* a threat can even reach the art.  
   - **Why it’s #1**: If an intruder bypasses this layer (e.g., through deception like the Gardner heist), all other defenses (alarms, displays) become reactive, not preventative.  
   - **Weakness Addressed**: Directly counters human error (e.g., guards falling for scams) by enforcing strict protocols like ID verification and restricted keycard access.  

2. **Alarms & Sensors (Motion Detectors, Glass Breaks)**  
   - **Role**: Immediate alerts if entry is breached.  
   - **Limitation**: They *react* to intrusion but don’t *prevent* it. Human error (e.g., delayed response) can still allow theft.  

3. **Secure Displays & Mountings**  
   - **Role**: Makes theft physically difficult *after* entry.  
   - **Limitation**: Useless if the intruder is already inside (e.g., Gardner’s thieves dismantled frames with tools).  

### Why Access Control is the True "First Line"  
The Gardner heist exemplifies this: thieves exploited the **human trust** at the entrance. Without robust access control (e.g., biometric locks, mandatory bag scans), even high-tech sensors and displays couldn’t have stopped the initial breach.  

**Modern Best Practice**:  
- Combine **layered human + tech controls**:  
  - **Perimeter guards** with clear protocols (e.g., "no entry without ID").  
  - **Automated bag checks** at all doors.  
  - **Keycard systems** for non-public areas (e.g., storage).  
- **Redundancy**: If guards fail, sensors still trigger alarms to delay thieves.  

In essence: **Prevent entry first. Secure the art second.** Human factors *must* be engineered out of the first line.
