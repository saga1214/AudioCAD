You've hit on a crucial nuance. The user is absolutely right that the Portuguese were already actively developing sea routes around Africa, making the Age of Discovery a *process* rather than a single event. The fall of Constantinople wasn't the *only* cause—it was more like a massive catalyst that dramatically accelerated and focused the effort.

Here's why your point is valid and how it fits with the bigger picture:

1.  **Pre-existing Momentum:** The Portuguese (and others) were already testing the waters, sailing down the African coast, seeking a route to Asia. The Age of Discovery was *already beginning*.
2.  **Constantinople as the Accelerator, Not the Sole Spark:** While trade wasn't "immediately closed" in 1453, the Ottoman capture of Constantinople:
    *   **Demonstrated the Threat:** It proved the Ottomans were a major power controlling the key land bridge and the eastern terminus of the most established overland route to Asia.
    *   **Heightened Urgency:** It made the risk and cost of relying on Venetian/Genoese intermediaries *much* higher. European monarchs felt a stronger imperative to find direct sea routes for wealth, glory (competitive rivalry you mentioned earlier), and spreading Christianity.
    *   **Provided a Symbolic Turning Point:** It became a powerful rallying cry and a justification for massive investment in risky voyages of exploration.
3.  **The Sequence Matters:** The Portuguese voyages down the African coast (like Dias rounding the Cape of Good Hope in 1488) gained *new urgency and backing* *after* 1453. The fall of Constantinople provided the critical impetus that turned these early explorations into the full-blown, state-sponsored Age of Discovery we know, culminating in da Gama reaching India in 1498.

**In essence:** You're correct. The Age of Discovery was a long-term process initiated by technological advances and the desire for wealth/glory/spread of faith. The Portuguese were already at the forefront. **However, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was the critical event that dramatically intensified the search, provided the political impetus for massive state funding, and marked a decisive shift in European strategy towards finding a direct sea route to bypass the now-threatening Ottoman gatekeepers.** It didn't *start* the process, but it acted as the ignition that turned the spark into a firestorm.
