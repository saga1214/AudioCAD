You've hit on a crucial nuance! While the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was a *major catalyst* and a powerful *catalyst* for urgency, it wasn't the *sole* cause of the Age of Discovery. You're absolutely right about the Portuguese momentum:

1.  **Portuguese Initiative:** By the 1430s-40s, Portugal was already heavily invested in exploring the African coast under Prince Henry the Navigator. They were developing shipbuilding (caravels), navigation (astrolabe, compass, improved cartography), and establishing trading posts.
2.  **The *Accelerator* Effect:** The fall of Constantinople in 1453 wasn't just about spice routes; it was a massive geopolitical shock. It signaled:
    *   **Threat to Existing Routes:** The Ottomans now controlled the Bosporus Strait, the key land gateway to the Black Sea and overland routes to Asia.
    *   **Risk and Instability:** Control over trade was becoming harder to maintain, prices could fluctuate wildly, and the route was dangerous.
    *   **A Call to Action:** Powerful Iberian kingdoms (Spain, Portugal, later England/France) saw this as a challenge to their wealth, glory (Catholic expansion), and access to knowledge. Finding a *direct, independent sea route* to Asia became an urgent national priority *beyond* just avoiding the Ottomans.

**So, to your points:**

*   **"Lead to"?** Yes, the Constantinople fall was a *major contributing factor* and a powerful *catalyst*.
*   **"Search for new sea routes to Asia... blocked traditional land routes":** Constantinople *was* the critical land gate. Its loss meant the overland route was now directly threatened or controlled by a rival power.
*   **"Search for direct access... made possible by advances... Powerful European kingdoms competing":** All accurate.
*   **"Venetian merchant logs showing Alexandria was the main port":** Correct. Alexandria was the *sea* hub. Constantinople was the *land* hub. The Ottomans threatened *both*.
*   **"Trade wasn't immediately closed... Portuguese African route already underway":** **Exactly.** This is the key nuance you're pointing out.
*   **"Would it have happened at the same time regardless?":** **Highly likely, yes.** The Portuguese momentum, technological readiness, and the *inherent* desire for new wealth, glory, and exploration would likely have led to significant overseas voyages *eventually*. However, the fall of Constantinople dramatically *accelerated* the focus on finding a *direct sea route to Asia* and intensified the competition between European powers (like Spain funding Columbus partly to counter Portuguese advances in Africa). It turned a potentially long-term project into an urgent race.

**In essence:** The Age of Discovery was already brewing due to Portuguese progress and European ambitions. The fall of Constantinople wasn't the spark that lit the fire; it was the massive shock that threw gasoline on it, focusing the race towards Asia and intensifying the competition for global dominance. It was a pivotal *trigger*, not the *only* cause.
