For modern-day art museum security, **controlling access at every entry point** remains the definitive first line of defense. This foundational layer prioritizes *prevention* before threats enter the building. Key components include:  

1. **Visible Security Presence**: Armed or trained guards deter potential intruders and respond immediately to anomalies.  
2. **Access Screening**: Mandatory bag checks, X-ray machines, and sometimes facial recognition or RFID screening to identify prohibited items or threats. Keycard access to restricted staff zones reinforces this but is secondary to public entry protocols.  

### Why This is the First Line:  
- **Proactive Deterrence**: High-profile surveillance and screening discourage attacks before they occur.  
- **Barrier Against Initial Breach**: The Gardner Museum heist (1990) succeeded partly due to guards overriding protocol—a flaw exploited at the *entry* point. Modern access controls mitigate such human-error risks.  
- **Layered Security Foundation**: All subsequent layers (cameras, alarms, secure displays) rely on restricting initial access to contain threats effectively.  

### Supporting Layers:  
- **Second Line**: Integrated surveillance (AI-powered cameras), motion detectors, and panic alarms.  
- **Third Line**: Reinforced display cases, anti-tamper alarms, and secure mounting systems to protect art *in transit* or *during* a breach.  

### Key Insight for Your Paper:  
While technology like secure displays is vital, **human-controlled access points are the critical first threshold**. A failure here allows intruders to bypass all internal defenses. Modern best practices combine strict access protocols with training to address human vulnerabilities—making this line both technological and behavioral.  

For further heist analysis, the **1990 Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum heist** remains the benchmark, underscoring how *initial access control* is paramount.
