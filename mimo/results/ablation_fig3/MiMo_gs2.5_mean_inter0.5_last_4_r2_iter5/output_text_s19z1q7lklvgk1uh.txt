You've highlighted a fascinating paradox: **there is no single "most important" step**. Making a sword is a complex process where every step is interdependent, and each is critically important. The "most important" element depends on your perspective:

1.  **Heat Treatment (Hardening/Tempering):** As you said first, this gives the steel its essential properties – hardness for an edge and toughness to survive impact. A flawed heat treat makes the blade useless or dangerous, regardless of other steps.
2.  **Materials:** The right steel is the foundation. The quality, type (high-carbon, pattern-welded, etc.), and condition of the material dictate the blade's potential.
3.  **Knowledge/Design Understanding:** As you emphasized, understanding balance, purpose (cutting, thrusting, parrying), metallurgy, and historical context separates a functional weapon from a "sword-shaped object."
4.  **Execution/Skill:** Whether using forging (like Larrinburg) or CNC (like your friend), the skill of the maker in executing the design, controlling the process, and ensuring precision is paramount. CNC removes forging skill but demands precise programming and setup skill.
5.  Laying the Blade / Fullering: Properly shaping the hollow of the blade (the fullering) is crucial for weight reduction, balance, and structural integrity. A poorly laid blade will warp or fail.
6.  Rounding the Edges / Beveling: Creating a sharp edge requires careful grinding and honing. A dull blade won't hold well.

**The Synthesis:**

*   **Heat Treatment, Materials, and Design Knowledge are the absolute bedrock.** You cannot succeed without at least a *basic* understanding of these.
*   **Execution Skill is what transforms theory into a functional object.** It's the bridge between the plan and the finished sword.
*   **CNC vs. Forging:** The method (CNC or forge) is a *tool choice*. Both can produce excellent swords when the underlying principles (material, heat treat, geometry, design intent, execution skill) are mastered. Your friend's accidental success highlights that sometimes the *correct outcome* emerges from the process, even if not perfectly planned – but it underscores that the process itself (materials, geometry, heat treat) was fundamentally sound.
