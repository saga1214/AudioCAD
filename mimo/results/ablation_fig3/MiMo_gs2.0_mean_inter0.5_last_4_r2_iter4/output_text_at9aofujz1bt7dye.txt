You're absolutely right to question this nuance! While intuition might suggest *more* ice = *less* friction, the physics is more complex. **At temperatures around -10°C, the coefficient of friction is typically *slightly higher* than at -5°C, often in the range of 0.01 to 0.02.** Here's why:

1.  **Reduced "Quench Film":** The thin layer of liquid water that forms on ice near the blade temperature (due to the blade's heat and pressure) is crucial for friction reduction. At very cold temperatures (-10°C and below), this film becomes much thinner or disappears entirely.
2.  **Harder Ice:** Extremely cold ice is harder and less deformable. This means the blade can "dig in" slightly more during the push-off or when stopping, increasing friction compared to the smoother glide phase where the film helps.
3.  **Pressure Effects:** While pressure melts the ice locally, the *overall* effect at very low temperatures can shift friction upwards slightly.

**So, to quantify around -10°C:**

*   **Glide Phase:** The coefficient of friction is likely **0.01 - 0.015**. It's *not* as low as 0.005.
*   **Push/Stop Phase:** Due to the harder ice and potential blade digging, friction can be slightly higher, perhaps **0.015 - 0.02** or even a little more depending on the exact conditions and blade pressure.

**Key Takeaways:**

*   **Optimal Glide:** The *lowest* coefficients (around 0.01) occur at the typical competition temperatures of **-5°C to -7°C**, where the quench film is present but the ice is still reasonably hard.
*   **Warmer Ice (e.g., -2°C):** Friction increases slightly (0.02 - 0.06) due to the softer, more deformable ice surface.
*   **Extra Cold Ice (-10°C):** Friction *increases* slightly (0.01 - 0.02) primarily due to the reduced quench film and harder ice, making gliding feel less smooth. It's *not* as low as 0.005.

**Pressure's Role:** Remember, pressure significantly affects friction too. During the push-off, high pressure melts the ice, creating the quench film that *lowers* friction for the subsequent glide. At very cold temperatures, the effectiveness of this pressure-melting might be slightly reduced.
