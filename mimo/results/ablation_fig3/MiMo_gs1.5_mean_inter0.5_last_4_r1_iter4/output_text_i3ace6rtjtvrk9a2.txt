You're absolutely right to question this. Let's break it down clearly:

**Pronunciation:** "Saccade" is pronounced **suh-KAH-dee** (like "suh-KAH-dee"). Emphasis on the second syllable.

**The Eye vs. The Brain: A Critical Distinction**

1.  **The Eye (Sensor):**
    *   Acts like a biological camera sensor.
    *   Receives light and converts it into electrical signals.
    *   **It does NOT see continuously.** It has a finite temporal resolution. It can detect rapid changes, but it can't capture a truly continuous, high-resolution stream of visual information like a camera sensor with infinite sampling rate.
    *   It experiences **saccadic masking** during the brief (~100ms) period when the eye is moving between fixation points. During this time, visual input is suppressed or significantly reduced.

2.  **The Brain (Processor):**
    *   Receives the raw, incomplete signals from the eye.
    *   **It processes these signals continuously.**
    *   It performs critical tasks:
        *   **Filling the Gaps:** During saccades, the brain relies heavily on memory, prediction, and the visual field *before* and *after* the saccade to create the illusion of a stable, continuous visual scene. This is the "saccadic mask" – the brain actively suppresses the actual visual input *during* the saccade to prevent motion blur, and then fills in the missing information.
        *   **Temporal Blending:** It blends visual information over very short time intervals (milliseconds to tens of milliseconds) to create the perception of smooth motion and continuity.
        *   **Contextual Integration:** It uses surrounding context to infer what's happening where the eye isn't looking.

**Answering Your Core Question: Does the Eye See Continuously?**

*   **No.** The eye itself does not experience a continuous stream of visual information. It receives discrete snapshots (photons hitting the retina) and experiences brief breaks in input during saccades.
*   **Yes.** The *perception* of continuous vision is created by the brain. It continuously processes and synthesizes the incomplete, discontinuous signals from the eye (and other senses) to construct the seamless visual world we experience.

**Why 60 Frames Per Second Feels "Real"?**

*   When you watch video at 30 or 60 fps, the brain is doing the *same* thing it does during saccades: it's filling in the gaps between the discrete frames. The frame rate is the *sampling rate* of the eye's sensor.
*   **60 fps is close to the eye's natural temporal resolution and saccadic masking window.** At 60 fps, the brain has enough samples per second to minimize the need for significant interpolation or smoothing. It feels "real" because it closely matches the rate at which the eye naturally samples and the brain naturally processes motion.
*   **24 fps feels "cinematic" because it's lower than the brain's processing speed.** The brain has to do more interpolation and blending to create the smooth motion from fewer samples. This creates a slightly different, more stylized, less hyper-real visual experience – the hallmark of film.

**In Summary:**

*   **The eye** captures visual information in discrete samples and experiences interruptions (saccades).
*   **The brain** continuously *processes* and *synthesizes* these incomplete signals, including filling gaps during saccades, to create the **perception** of continuous, stable, and smooth vision.
*   The "most natural" frame rate (like 60 fps) is the one where the *sampling rate* (frames per second) is closest to the *processing rate* of the brain for motion. Higher frame rates (1000 fps) allow for slower-motion playback by providing more samples to interpolate down to a lower frame rate, revealing details the eye couldn't capture naturally.

Your distinction between "what the eye can see" and "what the brain processes" is spot on. The perception of continuity is a product of the brain's processing, not the eye's direct sampling.
