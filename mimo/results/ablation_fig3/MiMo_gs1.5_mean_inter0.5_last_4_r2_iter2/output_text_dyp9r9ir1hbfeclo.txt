You're absolutely right to double-check! While the **humerus** (upper arm), **scapula** (shoulder blade), and **clavicle** (collarbone) are the primary bones enabling shoulder rotation, the **fourth critical component** is the **rotator cuff muscles**.  

Here's a quick recap of how they work together:  

1. **Clavicle (Collarbone)**  
   - Connects the arm to the sternum.  
   - Stabilizes the scapula and provides a pathway for arm movement.  

2. **Scapula (Shoulder Blade)**  
   - Houses the **glenoid socket** where the humerus articulates.  
   - Rotates and retracts to position the arm.  

3. **Humerus (Upper Arm Bone)**  
   - Rotates within the glenoid socket.  
   - Performs internal/external rotation.  

4. **Rotator Cuff Muscles**  
   - **Supraspinatus**: Initiates humeral rotation outward.  
   - **Infraspinatus**: Performs external rotation.  
   - **Teres Minor**: Assists in external rotation.  
   - **Subscapularis**: Performs internal rotation.  
   - *These muscles* **stabilize the humerus** in the socket, enabling smooth, controlled rotation.  

### Why This Matters for Rotation:  
The shoulder is a **ball-and-socket joint** (glenohumeral joint), but it lacks deep stabilizing structures. The rotator cuff muscles compensate for this by:  
- Preventing dislocation.  
- Ensuring the humeral head stays centered in the socket during movement.  
- Facilitating precise rotation (e.g., lifting your arm overhead).  

### Signs of Poor Rotation:  
- **Limited mobility** (e.g., difficulty reaching behind your back).  
- **Pain** during rotation (especially internal rotation, which strains the subscapularis).  
- **Weakness** in lifting or overhead movements.  
- **Grinding/stiffness** when moving the arm.  

If your friend's soreness persists or worsens after rest, it could indicate a **rotator cuff strain**. Encourage them to consult a clinician if symptoms don’t resolve with rest or targeted exercises (like scapular stabilization drills).
