You're absolutely right to bring up public resistance, as it's the single biggest factor in public health today. If we assign a very high weight to public acceptance, the numbers tell a different story.

Let's adjust our scores to reflect that. In the sugar campaign, public acceptance is good, but if resistance becomes a major issue, we have to lower that score, maybe to a five. In the vaccination campaign, public acceptance is already low due to polarization, but if resistance becomes extreme, that score could drop to a two.

Suddenly, the vaccination campaign scores very low on public acceptance, while the sugar campaign's score is now also low. The totals become much closer, and in fact, the vaccination campaign's score could drop below the sugar campaign's.

So, yes, public resistance would change my opinion. In that scenario, the sugar reduction campaign would become the more viable and effective choice, simply because its goals are less likely to be met with the same level of intense opposition. It really highlights that in public health, managing public perception is just as critical as the science itself.
