You've hit on a crucial nuance that historians increasingly recognize. **You're largely correct: the fall of Constantinople in 1453 wasn't the *starting point* of the Age of Discovery, nor was it the *immediate* cause of its acceleration.** Here's a more refined breakdown:

### What Constantinople's Fall *Actually* Did:
1. **Symbolic Shock**: It signaled the collapse of the Christian Roman Empire and the rise of the Ottoman Empire—a formidable Islamic power controlling key trade routes.
2. **Strategic Threat**: The Ottomans began taxing and controlling overland routes through the Black Sea and Balkans, making access to Asian spices **more expensive, dangerous, and unpredictable**.
3. **Accelerator, Not Catalyst**: It intensified the urgency for European powers (especially Portugal and Spain) to find *alternative sea routes* to Asia. The spice trade was too lucrative to abandon, but the existing routes were becoming untenable.

### Why Trade Wasn't "Immediately Closed":
- **Venetian Dominance**: Venice maintained control over Alexandria and Zanzibar for decades after 1453. Their merchant networks and ships continued trading until Portuguese explorers (like Vasco da Gama in 1498) directly challenged their monopoly.
- **Portuguese Progress**: By 1453, Portuguese explorers like Bartolomeu Dias had already rounded the Cape of Good Hope (1488), proving Africa could be a viable route. The fall of Constantinople *gave them a geopolitical push* to complete the journey.
- **Technological Readiness**: Shipbuilding (caravel, lateen sails) and navigation (astrolabe, compass) were already advancing. The *means* to discover existed; the *motivation* grew stronger after 1453.

### Other Key Drivers:
1. **Technological Innovation**: Better ships, navigation tools, and navigation techniques made long voyages feasible.
2. **Royal Patronage**: Monarchs (e.g., Portugal's Manuel I, Spain's Ferdinand and Isabella) funded expeditions for wealth, power, and spreading Christianity.
3. **Economic Competition**: Nations sought new sources of income and global trade dominance.
4. **Geopolitical Rivalry**: Spain and Portugal, in particular, competed fiercely for prestige and territorial claims.

### Conclusion:
The fall of Constantinople was a **major catalyst** that heightened the urgency and political will to circumnavigate Africa, but it didn't *create* the Age of Discovery. It accelerated pre-existing trends:  
- Portuguese explorers were already testing African routes.  
- Technological advances made oceanic voyages possible.  
- European powers were hungry for wealth and glory.  

Constantinople’s fall made the *quest* for new routes more desperate, but the *Age of Discovery* was already underway—1453 just gave it a powerful push forward.
