You're absolutely right to clarify that. Let's break it down clearly:

1.  **The Three Primary Bones:** The three bones that form the bony structure of the shoulder joint and enable its movement are:
    *   **Humerus:** The upper arm bone. Its head fits into the shoulder socket.
    *   **Scapula (Shoulder Blade):** The large, flat bone on your back. It provides the socket (glenoid cavity) for the humeral head and houses the rotator cuff muscles.
    *   **Clavicle (Collarbone):** The S-shaped bone connecting the arm to the sternum. It provides stability and helps position the arm.

2.  **The Fourth Component: The Rotator Cuff Muscles:** While the humerus, scapula, and clavicle form the *bony* framework, the **rotator cuff** is a group of **four muscles and their tendons** that work *around* the shoulder joint. These muscles are:
    *   **Supraspinatus** (above the shoulder blade)
    *   **Infraspinatus** (below the shoulder blade)
    *   **Teres Minor** (below the shoulder blade)
    *   **Subscapularis** (on the front of the shoulder blade)

**In Summary:**

*   **Bony Structure for Rotation:** Humerus, Scapula, Clavicle (3 bones).
*   **Muscular Support for Rotation:** The Rotator Cuff (4 muscles).

So, when I initially said "three main bones," I was referring specifically to the bony components. The rotator cuff muscles are the crucial soft tissue structures that dynamically stabilize and enable smooth rotation around the bony joint. Your friend's soreness during dumbbell lateral raises could indeed be rotator cuff-related, especially if it's sharp, persistent, or prevents him from completing the range of motion. If it doesn't resolve with rest or proper form, he should consult a doctor or physical therapist.
