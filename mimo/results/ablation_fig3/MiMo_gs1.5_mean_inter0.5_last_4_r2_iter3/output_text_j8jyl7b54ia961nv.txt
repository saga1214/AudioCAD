You're absolutely right to bring that up. The issue of public acceptance is the deciding factor here.

If we give public acceptance a higher weight, the sugar reduction campaign scores much higher. Its public acceptance is good, maybe a six, and while changing behavior is difficult, that implementation score is low, a two. When you add in the high long-term impact and moderate cost-efficiency, the vaccination campaign's score becomes much lower. Its public acceptance is polarized, scoring only a three.

So, with that extra weight, the choice flips. The vaccination campaign becomes much more difficult to justify. This really highlights how the public office's role isn't just medical or economic, but also about maintaining public trust and legitimacy. A campaign that is seen as controversial, even if it's medically sound, can be a much riskier choice. In this scenario, the sugar campaign becomes the better option.
