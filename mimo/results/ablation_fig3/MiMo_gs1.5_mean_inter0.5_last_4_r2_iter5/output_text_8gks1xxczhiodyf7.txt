If forced to choose **one single factor**, I'd argue **easy access to highly processed, calorie-dense foods** remains the biggest top driver behind increasing obesity rates. Here’s why:

### Key Reasons:
1. **Overconsumption**: These foods are engineered to be hyper-palatable (craveable) yet low in satiety signals (protein, fiber, volume). People eat more without realizing it.
2. **Pervasive Availability**: They’re cheaper, more shelf-stable, and heavily marketed than whole foods, making them the default choice for many.
3. **Nutrient Deficiency**: They lack essential nutrients, leading to "empty calories" that don’t support satiety or metabolic health.
4. **Synergy with Inactivity**: Even if someone exercises, overeating processed foods can easily create a calorie surplus.

### But It’s Not Just Diet:
- **Physical inactivity** is a major co-driver (modern lifestyles reduce daily movement).
- **Stress/sleep disruption** affects hormones like cortisol and leptin, increasing hunger.
- **Genetics/environment** play roles, but processed foods exploit biological vulnerabilities (e.g., sugar cravings).

### Why This Factor Wins:
While portion control, exercise, and sleep matter, **processed foods make it biologically harder to maintain a calorie balance**. They’re the "default" in food environments, driving overconsumption passively. For example:
- A person eating unprocessed calorie-dense foods (e.g., nuts) *without* overeating might maintain weight if portion-controlled.
- But processed foods (e.g., chips, soda) are designed to make overeating effortless, even with moderate activity.

### Bottom Line:
Processed foods are the **primary lever** driving obesity because they disrupt the natural balance between calorie intake and expenditure. Addressing this (via policy, education, and personal choices) would have the broadest impact.
