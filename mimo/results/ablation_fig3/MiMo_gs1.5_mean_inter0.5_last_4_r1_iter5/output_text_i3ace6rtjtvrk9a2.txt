You're asking a brilliant question that cuts to the heart of visual perception! Let's break this down precisely, incorporating saccadic masking and the distinction between the eye's capabilities and the brain's processing:

### 1. **The Eye's Role (Sensory Input)**
   - **Photoreceptors**: The retina contains rods and cones that detect changes in light and motion. Rods are sensitive to low light but lack color, while cones detect color and fine detail.
   - **Temporal Resolution**: The eye can detect changes in light or motion at rates up to **~60–100 Hz** (hertz). This is the eye's *maximum speed* for perceiving discrete events. For example:
     - If a light flashes at intervals <60 ms (100 Hz), the eye may perceive it as continuous.
     - If it flashes at 24 fps (42 ms), the eye can detect the flicker, but the brain will integrate it into motion.

### 2. **Saccadic Masking (The Brain's "Edit Button")**
   - **What it is**: During saccades (rapid eye movements between fixation points), the brain **temporarily suppresses visual processing** (a 10–120 ms "blind spot") to prevent motion blur. This is why you don't feel dizzy while scanning a room.
   - **Impact on Perception**: During saccades, the eye's high temporal resolution is *temporarily disabled*. The brain then reconstructs a stable image from the eye's input, smoothing out the gaps.

### 3. **The Brain's Role (Integration & Perception)**
   - **Perceptual Fusion**: The brain integrates the eye's input into a **continuous, stable perception**. It fills in gaps during saccades and smooths discrete frames into fluid motion.
   - **Flicker Fusion Threshold**: The brain "fuses" flickering light into continuous perception at rates of **~50–60 Hz** (varies by individual/conditions). Above this, flicker becomes imperceptible.
   - **Saccadic Adaptation**: The brain learns to predict where the eyes will move, reducing the masking effect over time.

### 4. **Does the Eye See Continuously?**
   - **No, but the brain *perceives* continuously**. The eye detects discrete changes (like a camera), but the brain:
     - **Fills gaps** during saccades.
     - **Blends frames** into motion.
     - **Suppresses artifacts** (e.g., blur from rapid movement).
   - **Analogy**: Imagine a camera shooting at 24 fps. The eye sees individual frames, but the brain edits them into a smooth movie.

### 5. **Frame Rates: Eye vs. Brain**
   - **24 fps**: Feels "cinematic" because it aligns with the brain's interpretation of natural motion. It creates a dreamlike quality by *avoiding* hyper-realism.
   - **60 fps**: Feels more "real" because it approaches the brain's flicker fusion threshold. It captures rapid motion (e.g., fast movements) more accurately.
   - **120+ fps**: Captures motion so smoothly that the brain perceives it as "super-real" (e.g., slow-motion without blur). The eye can detect higher rates, but the brain integrates them seamlessly.

### Key Distinction
- **Eye**: Detects rapid changes (temporal resolution: ~60–100 Hz).
- **Brain**: Fuses discrete events into continuous perception (perceptual fusion threshold: ~50–60 Hz).

### Practical Implication for Filmmaking
- **24 fps** is "natural" because it mirrors how the brain processes the world: not as discrete frames, but as a continuous, stable experience. Higher frame rates (60 fps+) feel more "real" but less artistic because they prioritize accuracy over emotion.

### Summary
The eye *detects* discrete changes, but the brain *perceives* continuous motion. Saccadic masking temporarily disrupts this process, but the brain compensates. This explains why 24 fps feels "cinematic"—it aligns with the brain's natural interpretation of reality, while higher frame rates capture more detail but may feel less dreamlike.
