You're right to ask about that fourth component—the **glenoid cavity**—is the key anatomical feature enabling rotation. Here's a precise breakdown:

1. **Humerus**: Upper arm bone. Its head fits into the glenoid cavity.
2. **Scapula**: Shoulder blade. It houses the glenoid cavity and articulates with the humerus.
3. **Clavicle**: Collarbone. Stabilizes the shoulder girdle but doesn't directly participate in rotation.
4. **Glenoid Cavity**: The **shallow socket** on the scapula where the humeral head articulates. This is the critical "fourth" component for rotation.

### How It Works:
- The glenoid cavity is **shallow and rounded** (like a shallow bowl), allowing the humeral head to move in all directions:  
  - **Internal Rotation** (arm turns inward)  
  - **External Rotation** (arm turns outward)  
- The **rotator cuff muscles** (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) act as a "mucosa" to stabilize the humeral head within this shallow socket, preventing dislocation while enabling fluid motion.

### Clinical Relevance:
If the glenoid cavity is misaligned (e.g., due to injury or arthritis), rotation becomes limited and painful. Rotator cuff injuries disrupt stability, leading to **subluxation** (partial dislocation) or **instability**.

### Your Friend's Soreness:
Dumbbell lateral raises primarily engage the **supraspinatus** (part of the rotator cuff). Soreness here is common, but **persistent pain** could indicate:  
- **Tendinitis** (inflammation of the supraspinatus tendon)  
- **Rotator cuff impingement** (tendon irritation during overhead movements)  
→ **Recommendation**: If pain continues, advise him to stop the exercise and consult a physiotherapist/doctor to rule out injury.

Understanding this interplay (bones + glenoid cavity + rotator cuff) is essential for diagnosing and treating shoulder pathologies! 🧠💪
