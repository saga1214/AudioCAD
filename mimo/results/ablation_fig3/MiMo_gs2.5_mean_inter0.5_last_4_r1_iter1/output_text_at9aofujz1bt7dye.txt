At temperatures around minus ten degrees Celsius, the coefficient of friction is indeed very low, but it’s more likely to be in that 0.005 to 0.01 range, as opposed to the even lower 0.01 to 0.02 you might see at the slightly warmer rink temperatures. The key thing to remember is that at very cold temperatures, the friction is still extremely low, but it’s a bit higher than at the optimal rink temperatures. And you know, skaters actually prefer that slightly higher friction because it gives them more control during turns and landings. So while it might seem like less glide, it’s actually more stable!
