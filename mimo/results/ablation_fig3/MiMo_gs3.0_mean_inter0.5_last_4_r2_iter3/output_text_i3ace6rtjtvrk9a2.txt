Now the meat of it: Can the eye see continuously? Technically yes at the photon-level—the retina detects photons in rapid succession. But the eye's own neural machinery isn't passive recording; ganglion cells in the retina already perform temporal interpolation. When eyes remain fixed, photoreceptors exhibit temporal integration, blending stimuli over milliseconds. 

However, continuous vision breaks down when the eyes initiate saccades. During these micro-jumps (typically 20-100ms duration), retinal image shifts dramatically. Saccadic masking isn't just masking blur—it's cortical suppression of motion parallax data. The visual cortex actively discards the ~0.1 seconds of visual input during rapid eye movements because it's biologically uneconomical to process. 

This creates a fascinating paradox: The eye's photoreceptors *could* theoretically record saccadic motion (some studies show retinal ganglion cells encoding it), but the brain's predictive processing model overrides this. Perception remains continuous because the brain prioritizes stability over raw data fidelity. 

For filmmaking implications: This explains why 24fps film feels continuous—it matches the brain's temporal interpolation window. Video's higher fps feels "smoother" because it exceeds the masking threshold, but sacrifices the cinematic "dreamlike" quality by overwhelming the brain's stabilizer with excessive detail. 

The student's insistence on separating eye vs. brain capabilities is astute. In neuro-visual science, we'd say: The eye provides high-bandwidth but low-latency raw data streams, while the brain applies real-time compression and motion smoothing. Neither is "continuous"—the illusion of continuity emerges from their integrated system.
