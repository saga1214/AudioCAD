### The Difference: Eye vs. Brain Processing  
1. **Eye (Eye/Sensory Input):**  
   - The eye's photoreceptors (rods/cones) detect changes in light/motion rapidly.  
   - Photoreceptors respond to stimuli in discrete "phrases" (around 50–100 ms per frame).  
   - During a saccade, the eye physically moves, but photoreceptors still *detect* motion/blur. However, this data is incomplete/jerky.  

2. **Brain (Processing/Perception):**  
   - The visual cortex (especially V1) actively *编辑s* sensory input during saccades.  
   - **Saccadic masking** is a neural mechanism where the brain suppresses motion-processing for ~200 ms during eye jumps to prevent blur.  
   - The brain fills gaps between saccades using "predictive interpolation," creating a *continuous* perceptual experience.  

---

### Saccadic Masking: The Key Mechanism  
- **What it is:** A temporary suppression of motion perception during rapid eye movements (saccades).  
- **Why it happens:** Prevents motion blur from destabilizing your sense of scene coherence.  
- **Effect on perception:**  
  - Your brain *consciously perceives* motion as continuous, even though the eye's input is fragmented.  
  - You don’t "see" individual frames; you "construct" motion from the brain.  

---

### Eye vs. Brain: The Continuity Paradox  
| **Aspect**               | **Eye (Sensory Input)**                          | **Brain (Perception)**                          |  
|--------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|  
| **Data reception**       | Continuous light capture; detects motion/blur.   | Filters/suppresses saccadic blur (200 ms).      |  
| **Temporal resolution** | Limited by photoreceptor response (~50–100 ms).  | Interpolates motion between saccades.           |  
| **Perceived continuity** | No; detects discrete "phrases."                 | **Yes**; constructs fluid motion.              |

### Why 60 FPS Feels Like Reality  
- **Eye limitation:** Photoreceptors can’t process faster than ~60 Hz (some up to 75 Hz). Beyond this, flicker fuses.  
- **Brain’s role:** At 60 FPS, the brain receives *enough* discrete frames to reconstruct stable, smooth motion. Higher FPS (e.g., 120 FPS) doesn’t add perceptual "newness" beyond 60 FPS because the brain *already* fills gaps.  
- **Saccadic masking relevance:** Even if the eye detects faster motion (e.g., a hummingbird), the brain’s interpolation limits our conscious perception to ~60 FPS.  

---

### Practical Implications for Filmmaking  
- **35mm (24 FPS):** Feels "cinematic" because 24 FPS aligns with the brain’s interpolation sweet spot. It’s stylized but natural due to the brain’s smoothing.  
- **Video (30/60 FPS):** Higher FPS feels more "real-time" because the brain receives more data points, reducing perceived artifacts (e.g., motion blur in fast action).  
- **Saccadic masking tip:** If you film subjects moving quickly, 60 FPS can help reduce perceived blur *without* the brain masking it as aggressively as 24 FPS.  

---

### Conclusion  
- **Does the eye see continuously?** **No.** The eye detects motion in discrete phrases, with saccadic blur during eye jumps.  
- **Does the brain perceive continuity?** **Yes.** The brain edits/suppresses saccadic blur and interpolates motion, creating the illusion of seamless reality.  
- **Why 60 FPS feels natural?** The brain receives enough data to reconstruct motion smoothly, matching its own processing speed.  

This distinction is crucial for storytelling: film leverages the brain’s "artistry" (24 FPS), while video prioritizes data fidelity (60 FPS+). Saccadic masking explains why your eye *could* detect higher FPS, but your brain *perceives* continuity at ~60 FPS.
