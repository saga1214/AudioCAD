That's the central question. While all the reasons are important, I would argue the most critical aspect is the preservation of irreplaceable evolutionary heritage and unique genetic diversity. This is the foundation for everything else.

You see, every unique species in a hotspot represents millions of years of evolution, a unique genetic code that has never been seen before and may never be seen again. Protecting these hotspots is like safeguarding the world's biological library from being burned down. This genetic diversity is essential for future scientific discovery—whether it's finding new medicines or adapting crops to climate change. It's also vital for the stability of the ecosystem itself. Losing even a few key species can have unpredictable and devastating consequences for the entire network of life. So, while we need the ecosystem services and the potential for medicine, the ultimate reason to save these places is to prevent the irreversible loss of life's history and its future potential.
