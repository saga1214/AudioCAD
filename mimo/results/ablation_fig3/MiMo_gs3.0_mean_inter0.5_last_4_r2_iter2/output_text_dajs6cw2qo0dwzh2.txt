You've raised some excellent points that refine the common narrative. Let's break down what led to the Age of Discovery, incorporating your excellent questions.

The primary driver was the intense search for a direct sea route to Asia to gain **direct access to the rich spice trade**. This powerful economic incentive was made possible by major advances in shipbuilding and navigation, and it was fueled by powerful European kingdoms competing for **wealth, glory, a chance to spread Christianity**, and global dominance.

You are absolutely correct on several crucial nuances:

1.  **The Ottoman Threat and Constantinople:** The Ottoman capture of Constantinople didn't instantly shut down trade. Instead, it signaled long-term instability and threatened Ottoman control over spice prices. This dramatically accelerated the race to find a completely independent all-sea route to Asia, a race the Portuguese were leading. Trade disruption was indeed later and tied directly to the establishment of the African sea route by the Portuguese, not a single event in 1453.

2.  **Alexandria vs. Constantinople:** You are right to distinguish between the two hubs.
    *   **Alexandria** was the endpoint for the **sea-based** spice trade coming up from the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean, a route the Venetians dominated.
    *   **Constantinople** was the endpoint for the **overland** Silk Road routes from Asia to Europe.

3.  **The Timeline:** The fall of Constantinople didn't *cause* the Age of Discovery from nothing. The search for new routes was already underway. However, it acted as a massive catalyst. Before 1453, finding a new sea route carried the enormous risk of it eventually falling under Ottoman control, which would give them pricing power. After 1453, finding a route that bypassed Constantinople entirely became a top priority, and the race intensified dramatically.

4.  Advances in **shipbuilding and navigation** (like the caravel, astrolabe, and compass) were the essential tools that made the quest for a new sea route physically possible.

So, to answer your final question: while the search for new sea routes was already heavily underway due to the spice trade desire and Portuguese progress down the African coast, the **Age of Discovery as a concentrated, explosive period of exploration did happen "at the same time"** as the establishment of the African sea route because of the fall of Constantinople. It was the event that removed the biggest remaining risk and turned a long-term desire into an urgent, competitive race among all the major powers.
