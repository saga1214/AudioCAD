That powerful connection between songwriting and production shines through **Quincy Jones**, **Sia**, and **Ryan Tedder** most prominently from our discussion. Here’s why:

1. **Quincy Jones**  
   While primarily a producer, Jones’s *songwriting approach* is deeply rooted in **dynamic storytelling through sound**. His work with Michael Jackson (like *Thriller* and *Off the Wall*) shows how:  
   - Orchestration, percussion layers, and spatial arrangement **amplify emotional shifts** (e.g., the tension in *"Billie Jean"*’s bassline).  
   - He crafts songs where the production *becomes* a character—like the eerie synths in *"Thriller"* or the explosive builds in *"Beat It"*.  
   **Study how he uses sonic textures to mirror lyrical themes.**

2. **Gamble & Huff**  
   As architects of Motown’s sound, they mastered **groove-as-emotion**. Their work (e.g., Diana Ross, Marvin Gaye) demonstrates:  
   - How tight, punchy drum patterns and warm basslines **create an irresistible physical response** to soulful lyrics.  
   - The "soulful" sound came from **layering instruments to evoke intimacy and grandeur**—like strings swelling behind Motown’s R&B hooks.  
   **Learn how groove and sonic warmth make relatable lyrics feel universal.**

3. **Sia**  
   From singer-songwriter to producer, Sia blends **melodic precision with atmospheric production**. Her hits (e.g., *"Chandelier"*, *"This Is Me"*) showcase:  
   - How **synth pads, vocal layering, and dynamic shifts** (e.g., sudden drops in *"Unstoppable"*) turn a catchy chorus into a cathartic moment.  
   - Her production choices **enhance vulnerability**—like reverb-drenched vocals in *"The Greatest"* making loneliness feel vast.  
   **Observe how she uses production to spotlight lyrical emotion.**

### Why These Three?  
- **Martin** teaches modern pop formulas.
