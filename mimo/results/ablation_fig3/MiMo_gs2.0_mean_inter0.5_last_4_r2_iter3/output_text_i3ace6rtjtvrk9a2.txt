To answer your question directly: **No, it is not correct to say the human eye sees continuously.** Here's a precise breakdown using scientific terminology:

### 1. **Eye Function: Sensory Detection**
   - The eye acts as a **biological sensor**. Photoreceptors (rods/cones) detect **changes in light and movement patterns** very quickly (up to ~60–75 Hz flicker fusion threshold).  
   - During **saccades** (rapid eye movements between focal points), the eye detects intense blur and fragmented visual data.  
   - **No continuous image capture**: The eye does not record a stable, uninterrupted stream of light like a camera. Instead, it samples visual information in discrete bursts during saccades.

### 2. **Brain Function: Perception Construction**
   - The visual cortex **processes and interprets** signals from the eye.  
   - **Saccadic masking** is the brain’s active suppression of blur during saccades. It edits visual data to maintain the illusion of stability.  
   - The brain **fills gaps** between eye samples, smoothing jerky motion and creating the perception of continuous, stable vision.  
   - Without this processing, you would perceive rapid head/eye movements as jerky or unstable ("judder").

### Key Difference
| **Eye (Sensory)** | **Brain (Processing)** |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Detects rapid changes (up to ~60–75 Hz) | Smooths discontinuous eye data |
| Experiences saccadic blur during movement | Suppresses saccadic blur (saccadic masking) |
| Provides raw, unstable visual samples | Creates the perception of continuous motion |

### Conclusion
The eye **detects** visual information discontinuously (via saccades and photoreceptor responses), while the brain **processes** it to create the perception of continuous motion. **Saccadic masking is the brain’s mechanism for making discontinuous eye data feel seamless.** Thus, the eye does not see continuously—it’s the brain’s interpretation that gives us the illusion of continuity.  

This distinction is critical for filmmakers using 35mm: the 24 fps standard leverages the brain’s smoothing ability to mimic "reality," while higher fps (e.g., 60 fps) reveals more detail but can feel less cinematic without brain-level stabilization.
