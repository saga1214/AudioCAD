You're right to clarify - let's break down the shoulder's rotation mechanics precisely:

1.  **The Three Key Bones:**
    *   **Humerus:** Upper arm bone. Its spherical head fits into the shallow socket of the scapula, enabling the rotation.
    *   **Scapula (Shoulder Blade):** Provides the socket (glenoid fossa) for the humeral head and anchors the rotator cuff muscles.
    *   **Clavicle (Collarbone):** Connects the arm (humerus) to the axial skeleton (spongy bone of the sternum at the sternoclavicular joint). It acts as a lever for arm movement and helps stabilize the shoulder joint.

2.  **The Fourth Component Clarification:**
    *   **You mentioned three bones, but asked about a "fourth component" earlier.** This is a common point of confusion. **There is no fourth bone working *directly* with the humerus and scapula for rotation.**
    *   The clavicle is the **third bone** in the shoulder girdle triad (humerus, scapula, clavicle). It's crucial for overall shoulder stability and arm movement, but the *specific circular rotation* of the arm happens primarily between the humerus and the scapula.

3.  **How Rotation Works:**
    *   The humeral head rotates within the shallow socket of the scapula.
    *   The muscles of the rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) provide the power and stability for this rotation.
    *   The clavicle provides structural support and helps position the shoulder.

**Regarding Your Friend's Soreness:**

Yes, soreness during lateral raises is common, especially with heavier weights or improper form. It usually stems from the deltoid muscles (especially the middle and anterior heads) working hard. **However, if the soreness is:**

*   **Sharp or localized** (especially in the front or top of the shoulder)
*   **Persists beyond normal muscle fatigue (24-48 hours)**
*   **Worsens with specific movements** (like internal rotation)
*   **Feels like joint pain** (not just muscle)

...it could indicate rotator cuff irritation or strain, especially if he has underlying instability or weakness. **If the soreness is persistent or worsening, he should consult a doctor or physical therapist.**
