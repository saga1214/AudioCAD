That's an excellent question, and you're on the right track with those estimates! Based on research and observations in ice physics, here's how the coefficient of friction (COF) generally behaves at very cold rink temperatures around **-10°C (14°F)**:

1.  **Standard Competition Ice (approx. -5°C to -7°C):** ~0.01 to 0.02
2.  **Warmer Rink (approx. -2°C to -3°C):** ~0.02 to 0.06 (Higher due to softer ice/water layer)
3.  **Extremely Cold Ice (approx. -10°C to -12°C):** **~0.005 to 0.008**

**Why the drop at -10°C? (The Science)**

*   **Loss of the Lubricating Water Layer:** At standard rink temperatures, a thin, near-liquid layer of water forms on the ice surface due to a phenomenon called the "quasi-liquid layer" (QLL). This layer acts as a lubricant, drastically reducing friction during the *glide* phase of skating.
*   **Harder Ice:** At -10°C and below, the ice becomes significantly harder and less deformable.
*   **Reduced Pressure Melting:** The pressure exerted by the skate blade on the ice is lower at this temperature (since the blade isn't digging in as deeply), and the reduced temperature further suppresses the "pressure melting" effect that slightly softens the ice locally under the blade.
*   **Increased Contact Area:** Because the ice is harder and less deformable, the skate blade makes more actual contact with the solid ice surface itself during the push and glide phases, increasing friction compared to the glide at warmer temperatures.

**Why not even lower? (0.001 or less)**

*   While friction *does* increase at very cold temperatures, it doesn't drop to the extreme levels you might imagine (like 0.001). The fundamental properties of ice and steel, combined with the nature of contact, prevent it from becoming *that* slippery.
*   The values of **0.005 to 0.008** represent a significant reduction from standard rink conditions but are still measurable coefficients of friction.

**In Summary:**

*   At **-10°C**, the coefficient of friction between a skate blade and ice is typically estimated to be in the range of **0.005 to 0.008**.
*   This is **lower** than at standard competition temperatures (-5°C to -7°C) but **higher** than at warmer rink temperatures (-2°C to -3°C).
*   The primary reason for this behavior at -10°C is the combination of the **harder ice** and the **reduction in the lubricating quasi-liquid layer**, leading to more direct contact and higher friction compared to the glide phase at warmer temperatures.

This explains why rinks actively avoid temperatures this cold – the increased friction makes gliding noticeably harder and reduces the performance of skaters relying on smooth glide phases.
