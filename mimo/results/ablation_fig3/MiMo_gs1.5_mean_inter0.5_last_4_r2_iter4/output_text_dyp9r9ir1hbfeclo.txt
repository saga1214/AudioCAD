That's a great question for clarification! Let's revisit the shoulder anatomy correctly. **There are actually three primary bones involved in shoulder rotation, not four.** I may have misspoken earlier. The three key bones are:

1.  **Humerus:** Your upper arm bone. Its head rotates within the socket of the scapula.
2.  **Scapula (Shoulder Blade):** This flat, triangular bone provides the socket (glenoid cavity) for the humeral head and houses the rotator cuff muscles.
3.  **Clavicle (Collarbone):** This S-shaped bone connects the scapula to the sternum (breastbone) and provides attachment points for muscles crucial for shoulder movement and stability.

**The "Fourth Component" is Muscular Support:** While the three bones form the core structure, the **rotator cuff muscles** (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) are absolutely essential for enabling smooth, controlled rotation. They act like a "mousetrap" to hold the humeral head firmly in the shallow glenoid socket of the scapula, allowing the arm to rotate freely.

**In Summary:**

*   **Bones:** Humerus, Scapula, Clavicle.
*   **Muscles:** Rotator Cuff (4 muscles) provide the crucial stabilizing force *around* these bones to make rotation possible and stable.
*   **Rotation:** The humerus rotates within the scapula's socket, guided and stabilized by the rotator cuff muscles. The clavicle helps anchor the scapula to the body.

So, while the bones are the hardware, the rotator cuff muscles are the critical software (or stabilizers) that allow for effective rotation. Soreness during lateral raises *can* definitely be related to overuse or strain of these muscles or the bones if form is poor.
