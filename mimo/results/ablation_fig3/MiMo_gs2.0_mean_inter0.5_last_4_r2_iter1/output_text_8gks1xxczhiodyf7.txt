If I had to choose **one single factor**, I'd say the **easy access to highly processed, calorie-dense foods** remains the biggest driver behind rising obesity rates. Here’s why:

### Why Processed Foods Stand Out:
1. **Hyper-Palatability**: These foods are engineered to override natural satiety signals. They’re high in sugar, fat, salt, and refined carbs, making them extremely addictive and overconsumable.
2. **Low Nutrient Density**: They provide calories without essential nutrients (protein, fiber, vitamins), leading to prolonged hunger and increased cravings.
3. **Portion Inflation**: Pre-packaged foods and restaurant meals are often designed for larger portions, making it easy to exceed calorie needs without realizing it.
4. **Convenience**: They’re cheap, accessible, and time-saving, especially in low-income areas, making them a default choice for many.

### Why Other Factors (Like Exercise) Aren’t the Top Driver:
- While **physical inactivity** contributes to obesity, it’s often a *consequence* of the food environment. For example:
  - Sedentary lifestyles are linked to processed-food consumption.
  - Convenience foods enable prolonged sitting (e.g., eating while working or watching TV).
- Even if someone exercises, consistently overeating processed foods can lead to weight gain. Conversely, someone eating unprocessed calorie-dense foods (e.g., nuts, avocados) in controlled portions is less likely to gain weight without exercise.

### Key Evidence:
- Studies show that **processed-food consumption directly predicts obesity rates** across countries, independent of income or education.
- When processed foods are restricted, obesity rates drop significantly (e.g., in countries with fewer ultra-processed-food availability).

### Bottom Line:
Processed foods create a **systemic imbalance** between calorie intake and expenditure. While exercise, genetics, and sleep matter, the pervasive availability of hyper-palatable, nutrient-poor foods is the *primary* catalyst for the modern obesity epidemic. Addressing this through policy (e.g., taxes on sugary drinks, labeling reforms) has proven more impactful than focusing solely on individual behavior change.
