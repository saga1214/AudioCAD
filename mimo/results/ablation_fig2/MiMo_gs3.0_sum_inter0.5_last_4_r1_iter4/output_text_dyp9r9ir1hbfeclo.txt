You've hit on an important clarification! While three **bones** work *directly* together for rotation (humerus, scapula, clavicle), the **fourth component** people often refer to is actually the **rotator cuff muscles**, not another bone. Here's the breakdown:

1.  **Three Bones (Structural Framework):**
    *   **Humerus:** Upper arm bone.
    *   **Scapula:** Shoulder blade (shallow socket holds the humeral head).
    *   **Clavicle:** Collarbone (stabilizes the scapula's position relative to the sternum/ribs).

2.  **Four Muscles (Functional "Rotator Cuff" - Not Bones):**
    *   **Supraspinatus:** Initiates lift-off from the socket.
    *   **Infraspinatus:** External/abduction rotation.
    *   **Teres Minor:** External/abduction rotation.
    *   **Subscapularis:** Internal/adduction rotation.
    *   *(Note: These are tendons originating on the scapula and attaching to the humerus, not separate bones)*.

**Regarding Your Friend's Lateral Raise Soreness:**

Yes, this is absolutely something to watch for! Lateral raises primarily work the **deltoid muscle** (front shoulder), but they heavily recruit the **rotator cuff muscles** (especially supraspinatus and infraspinatus) to stabilize the humeral head within the scapula's socket.

*   **Normal Soreness:** Mild soreness the next day is common for any new or intense exercise and usually resolves quickly.
*   **Concerning Soreness:** Sharp, stabbing, or persistent pain *during* or *after* the exercise, especially if it worsens with overhead movements or limits shoulder range of motion, is a classic sign of **rotator cuff irritation or strain**. This pain often radiates down the arm.

**Key Takeaway:** While the three bones provide the structure, the **rotator cuff muscles** are the essential fourth component working *with* those bones to enable smooth, controlled rotation. Your friend's soreness warrants careful observation. If the pain is sharp, persistent, or limits function, he should consult a doctor or physical therapist to rule out injury.
