That's a really important question. There's no single "most important" aspect; it's really a combination of interconnected factors.

First, you have the immense, irreplaceable value of unique species. As Norman Myers himself later argued, our main focus should be on saving the plants that are unique to these regions because animals can't survive without their food and habitat.

Then there's the potential for new medicines. The unique chemical compounds in these plants hold the key to new drugs. The potential loss is just... enormous.

And you also have to consider the cultural and ethical side. For millions of people, these places are sacred, they're sources of food, and they're part of their heritage. Losing them would be a huge cultural catastrophe.

Finally, there's the bigger picture of planetary health. Protecting these hotspots helps maintain the overall balance of the Earth's systems, like climate regulation and nutrient cycles, which we all depend on.

So, really, it's the combination of all these things—the unique species, the future medicines, the cultural value, and the global ecological stability—that makes preserving hotspots so critical.
