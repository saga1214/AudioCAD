### Key Factors at Very Low Temperatures:
1. **Pressure Melting**:  
   At typical rink temperatures (-5°C to -10°C), sharp blades exert pressure on ice, causing localized melting and lubricating water to form. This drastically reduces friction.  
   **→ At -10°C or colder**, ice is harder, and this pressure-induced melting *reduces*. Less lubricating water means friction rises slightly.

2. **Surface Texture**:  
   Warmer ice (near -5°C) may have microscopic cracks or unevenness, increasing friction. At -10°C, the ice surface becomes smoother and more uniform, but the lack of lubrication still dominates.

3. **Blade Sharpness & Pressure**:  
   Elite skaters use ultra-sharp blades to maintain low friction. At extreme cold, the blade’s ability to "cut" through the ice decreases, reducing pressure melting and increasing friction.

### Practical Implications:
- **Skater Technique**: At -10°C, skaters often push harder to compensate for slightly higher friction, though gliding remains effortless compared to friction surfaces like wheels on asphalt (≈0.01–0.02).

- **Competition Ice**: Olympic venues often target -6°C to balance low friction with safety (too cold risks injury from increased friction/stops).

### Comparison to Lake Ice:
Outdoor lake ice (unlike engineered rink ice) is rougher and less consistent. Friction typically ranges from **0.05–0.15** due to uneven surfaces and impurities—far higher than rink ice.
