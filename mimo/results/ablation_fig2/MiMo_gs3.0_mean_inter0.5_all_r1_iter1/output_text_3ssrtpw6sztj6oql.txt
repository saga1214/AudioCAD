For your modern-day security paper on an art museum, the **unambiguous first line of defense is controlling access at every entry point**. This foundational layer prioritizes **human-centric security controls** to prevent threats from entering the museum in the first place. Here’s a breakdown of why it’s critical:  

1. **Visible Security Presence (Guards)**: Deters intruders through a physical, authoritative barrier at doors, entrances, and loading docks.  
2. **Screening Procedures (Bag Checks)**: Limits what can enter, reducing risks like weapons or explosives. Mandatory ID checks for staff also apply.  

### Why This Is Primary, Not Technology:  
While systems like surveillance cameras, motion detectors, and secure display cases are vital, they function as **secondary layers**. They detect or contain breaches *after* an intruder bypasses the perimeter. The Gardner heist (1990) exemplifies this: the thieves exploited human error (deceiving guards) to enter, rendering internal alarms and displays irrelevant until it was too late. Controlling access *prevents* the initial breach.  

### Modern Evolution of This Line:  
- **Automated Turnstiles**: Restrict crowding.  
- **Biometric Scanners**: For VIP/staff access.  
- **Perimeter Alarms**: Motion sensors, lasers, or drones to detect perimeter intrusion *before* entry.  

In summary, for art museums, **physical and procedural barriers at entry points are the non-negotiable first line**. They address the root vulnerability: *who and what gets inside*.
