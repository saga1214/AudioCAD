That's a great question. It's really a multi-layered issue, so there isn't one single, simple answer. The most important aspect really depends on the specific context and the goals.

For Norman Myers and the conservation field that grew from his work, the core definition itself is the most important aspect—that dual requirement of high unique biodiversity and severe human threat. It's a framework for identifying critical areas.

For the hundreds of millions of people who depend on these ecosystems for their survival, the most important aspect is the tangible, practical ecosystem services they provide—like water, food, and climate stability.

For scientists, it's about preserving the irreplaceable genetic library of unique species, which holds the key to future medicines and technologies we can't even imagine yet.

And for global policymakers, hotspots are a strategic conservation tool. They help prioritize limited resources to have the maximum global impact.

So, in essence, the most important aspect is ensuring that these irreplaceable ecosystems continue to function, not just because they're beautiful, but because their survival is deeply connected to the well-being of people and the health of the planet.
