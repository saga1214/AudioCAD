That's a great question about the extremes! While the coefficient of friction between skate blades and ice is famously low, **at temperatures around -10°C, it typically doesn't drop to 0.005–0.008.** Instead, it tends to **increase slightly** due to changes in ice properties:

### Key Factors at -10°C:
1. **Loss of the Quasi-Liquid Layer**:  
   At typical rink temps (-5 to -10°C), pressure from the skate blade melts a thin (~10–100 nm) layer of ice, creating a lubricating water film.  
   **At -10°C**, this layer becomes thinner and less effective, reducing the "slippery" effect.

2. **Increased Ice Rigidity**:  
   Ice becomes harder and more crystalline at very low temperatures. This means blades "dig in" more instead of gliding smoothly, increasing friction.

3. **Friction Range at -10°C**:  
   Experiments suggest the coefficient is likely **closer to 0.02–0.05** at -10°C, not 0.005–0.008.  
   - *Example*: High-speed cameras and force sensors show skaters experience ~10–20% higher friction at -10°C vs. -7°C.

### Why the Confusion?
- **Pressure Melting Still Occurs**:  
  Blades *do* still melt ice under pressure (via the **Clausius-Clapeyron relation**), but the effect weakens as temperature drops.  
- **Ice Surface Changes**:  
  At -10°C, ice surfaces develop more defects and impurities, increasing resistance.

### Practical Implications:
- **Skaters Adjust Technique**:  
  Elite skaters use more pressure when ice is extremely cold to compensate for reduced friction.  
- **Rink Maintenance**:  
  Competitions avoid temperatures below -10°C because gliding becomes harder, affecting spins, turns, and jumps.

### Real-World Data:
- Studies (e.g., *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2018) show friction peaks around **0.03–0.04** at -10°C, then declines again below -15°C (where ice becomes more "brittle" and slippery in unexpected ways).

So while the friction is still very low compared to rubber-on-wood (0.4) or metal-on-metal (0.2), it’s **not** in the 0.005–0.008 range at -10°C. The real magic happens at **-7°C to -9°C**, where the quasi-liquid layer is optimal for effortless gliding! 🚥⛸️
