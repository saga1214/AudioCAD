Alright, medical exam refresher, got it. You're focused on shoulder bones for rotation. Three primary players work together directly: First, the Humerus—that's your upper arm bone, the long one connecting to your elbow. Second, the Scapula, the flat, triangular shoulder blade that sits on your upper back. And third, the Clavicle, the slender, S-shaped bone under your collarbone that links the arm to the scapula. These three fuse dynamically for that wide-rotational freedom. The humeral head fits into a shallow socket on the back of the scapula—that key joint enables circular rotation. The clavicle adds crucial stability.

Rotation specifically means the humerus spinning on its own axis—like turning your arm inward toward your torso (internal rotation) or outward away from it (external rotation). This almost exclusively happens where the humerus meets the scapula.

The "rotator cuff" adds muscle support. It comprises four muscles and their tendons: The Supraspinatus sits above the shoulder blade, initiating the lift. The Infraspinatus and Teres Minor wrap around the posterior scapula, handling external rotation. The Subscapularis anchors under the scapula, managing internal rotation. Together, they stabilize the humerus in its socket and enable smooth arm movements.

Poor rotation manifests as stiffness making circular motions difficult, a reduced range of motion (especially in abduction or rotation), painful internal rotation, weakness when lifting laterally, and persistent nighttime or overhead pain.

That fourth component you asked about? Exercise-induced soreness like your friend's from lateral raises often stems from muscle fatigue in the deltoids and supraspinatus. It's normal. But persistent sharp pain, weakness, or loss of motion could signal rotator cuff strain or impingement. If it recurs, medical evaluation is wise.

The three bones collaborating for rotation in the shoulder are:  
1. **Humerus** (upper arm bone)  
2. **Scapula** (shoulder blade)  
3. **Clavicle** (collarbone)  

The fourth component related to shoulder function is the **rotator cuff**, which comprises four muscles/tendons:  
- **Supraspinatus** (initiates arm lift)  
- **Infraspinatus** (external rotation)  
- **Teres minor** (external rotation)  
- **Subscapularis** (internal rotation)  

These bones and muscles work together to enable smooth, multi-axial rotation of the arm. Poor rotation often manifests as stiffness, reduced range of motion, weakness, or pain during overhead/lateral movements.
