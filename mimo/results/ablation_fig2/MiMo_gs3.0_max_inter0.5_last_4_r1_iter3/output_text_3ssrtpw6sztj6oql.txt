For a modern-day art museum, the **first and most critical line of defense remains physical and human-based control at every public entry point**.  

This foundational layer prioritizes **preventing unauthorized access** through:  
1. **Visible security presence** (armed guards or trained personnel) to deter threats and establish immediate deterrence.  
2. **Strict access protocols** (e.g., bag checks, ID verification, turnstiles) to filter individuals and objects before they enter the museum.  
3. **Controlled entry mechanisms** (keycard-operated doors, anti-ram barriers) to limit who can manage access points.  

While human error (as highlighted in infamous heists like the Gardner Museum robbery) can be exploited, this line remains the **initial strategic barrier**. Technology like sensors and alarms operates *within* this layer to amplify deterrence and response, but the *first* line is always about **managing who enters the building**.  

### Why This is Non-Negotiable:  
- **Proactive Deterrence**: Guards and screening procedures disrupt potential intruders *before* they bypass deeper defenses.  
- **Layered Strategy**: Even if breached, this line creates time for internal systems (alarms, secure displays) to activate.  
- **Modern Evolution**: Access control now integrates biometrics, AI-powered surveillance feeds at entrances, and automated screening systems—but the *objective* remains unchanged: **control entry**.  

The Gardner heist underscores this: the thieves exploited the *human* element of entry (fooling guards), proving that robust access control is the essential first line to mitigate such risks.
