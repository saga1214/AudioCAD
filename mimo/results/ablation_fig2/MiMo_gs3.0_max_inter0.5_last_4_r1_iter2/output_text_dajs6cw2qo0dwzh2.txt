You've hit on a crucial nuance! While the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was a **major catalyst**, it's more accurate to see the Age of Discovery as a **complex interplay of multiple factors** that converged around that time. Here's a breakdown of your points:

1.  **Constantinople's Fall (1453):** This was a **significant accelerant**, not the sole spark. It dramatically intensified the European desire for a direct sea route to Asia:
    *   **Blocked Gateway:** It severed the vital overland Silk Road access to spices controlled by the Ottomans, making spices vastly more expensive and less available.
    *   **Symbol of Instability:** It signaled Ottoman power and the threat of further expansion, adding urgency to finding alternative routes.
    *   **Psychological Impact:** It demonstrated the vulnerability of established trade routes and the need for bold, independent action.

2.  **Existing Portuguese Innovations:** You are absolutely correct. The Portuguese were already pushing the boundaries:
    *   **Advances:** They had pioneered significant improvements in shipbuilding (caravel) and navigation (astrolabe, compass, more accurate maps).
    *   **Early Ventures:** Explorers like Henry the Navigator and later Bartolomeu Dias had already begun exploring the West African coast, establishing trading posts and gathering crucial navigational knowledge.
    *   **The Route:** Dias proving the Atlantic was navigable and reaching the Cape of Good Hope (1488) provided the blueprint for rounding Africa to reach Asia.

3.  **The Search for Spices Remained the Core Driver:** Regardless of *when* Constantinople fell, the **overwhelming economic motivation** was the desire for direct access to the incredibly profitable spice trade (pepper, cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg). Rival kingdoms (Portugal, Spain, England, France, Netherlands) competed fiercely for wealth, glory (gold), territorial expansion, spreading Christianity, *and* controlling these lucrative trade routes.

**Conclusion:**

*   **Constantinople's fall was a major catalyst:** It massively increased the perceived *urgency* and *necessity* for a sea route to Asia, making the Portuguese exploration of Africa seem even more vital.
*   **Technology was essential:** Without the caravel and improved navigation, reaching Africa or the Americas would have been impossible.
*   **Rivalry provided the engine:** The competition between powerful European monarchies (funded by state backing) provided the resources and willpower to undertake these enormous, risky expeditions.
*   **Spice Trade was the prize:** This was the ultimate economic goal driving the entire enterprise.

**So, to your specific question:** Yes, the Age of Discovery would have likely happened *sooner* because of the relentless pursuit of the spice trade and the existing technological progress pioneered by the Portuguese. However, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was a **critical tipping point** that dramatically accelerated the efforts, validated the Portuguese route as the *only viable alternative*, and intensified the global competition that defined the era. It transformed a long-term goal into an urgent, continent-spanning race.
