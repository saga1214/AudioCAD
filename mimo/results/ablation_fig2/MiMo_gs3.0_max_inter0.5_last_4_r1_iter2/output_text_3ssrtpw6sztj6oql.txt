For modern-day art museum security, the **unshakably foundational first line of defense must be controlling all physical entry points**, as this is the primary interface between the museum and the external environment. This is the most critical layer because:

1. **Prevention Over Detection**: It aims to *prevent* unauthorized access before security incidents occur, rather than reacting to breaches.  
2. **Human & Environmental Vulnerability**: As the Gardner heist proved, human error (e.g., trusting imposters) is a universal risk. A robust first line mitigates this by automating or standardizing access.  
3. **Modern Threat Evolution**: Attacks increasingly target entry points (e.g., tailgating, distraction tactics). A layered entry strategy neutralizes these threats at the outset.  

### Key Components of Modern Entry Control:
- **Visible Security Presence**: Guards to deter threats and manage crowds.  
- **Automated Screening**: Bag checks, X-ray machines, and metal detectors to identify weapons or contraband.  
- **Access-Managed Entry Systems**: Turnstiles, biometric scanners, or encrypted keycard/pin systems to track and limit access.  
- **Perimeter Deterrence**: Motion sensors, floodlights, and barriers (e.g., bollards) to prevent vehicle intrusions.  

### Why This is Non-Negotiable:
Artifacts like Vermeers or Picassos are irreplaceable. If an intruder bypasses this layer, *all* subsequent defenses (cameras, alarms, secure cases) become reactive. The Gardner heist succeeded because the first line failed—**modern security prioritizes making entry the hardest step in the entire operation.**
