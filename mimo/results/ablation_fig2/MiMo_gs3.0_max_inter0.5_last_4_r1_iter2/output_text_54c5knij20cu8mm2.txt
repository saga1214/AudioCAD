Sure, let’s start with trypological dyslexia. It means difficulty decoding clusters of small holes. Reading challenges arise from trouble separating sound units from words. For example, with “laugh,” someone with this type may sound it out as “luh-gg” instead of recognizing the correct pronunciation. 

Now, surface dyslexia is different. It involves difficulty recognizing whole words by sight, especially irregular words. English has many such words, like “yacht” or “colonel.” Someone with surface dyslexia relies heavily on phonetic decoding, so when they see an irregular word like “laugh,” it doesn’t sound right, and they get stuck. They may read “yacht” as “ya-cht.” They can handle phonetically regular words fine, but irregular ones trip them up. 

Visual dyslexia involves trouble consistently seeing letters in the right order, so they might see “laugh” as “laguh” or something similar because the visual form confuses them. 

As for reasoning styles, deductive reasoning starts with general principles and applies them to specifics, like inferring that all birds have feathers because a robin is a bird. Inductive reasoning gathers specific observations to form general principles, like inferring that all birds have feathers from observing robins, sparrows, and parrots. It’s not a myth, but more about how people process information differently. 

Regarding other reasoning types, yes, there's more. Abductive reasoning is forming the most likely explanation from incomplete information, like a doctor diagnosing a condition from symptoms. Analogical reasoning draws conclusions from similarities between different situations. 

So, in summary, while we know these reasoning methods exist, there’s no definitive “either/or” wiring in the brain. It’s more nuanced.
