The Red team's forward, number nine, is in an offside position behind the second-to-last defender when a teammate, number eleven, plays a through ball. The ball is played just before number nine touches it, so he is definitely in an offside position when the ball is played.

Now, player number nine immediately turns and runs backward, away from the play, without trying to get involved. The ball continues forward and is intercepted by a Blue defender. That defender then plays the ball back to the Red player number nine, who is now in an onside position. Number nine controls the ball and then turns and scores.

College certification exam: You would call this an offside offense. The reason is that the player was in an offside position when their teammate played the ball, and they got involved in the play later by receiving that ball from the defender. For the Blue team, the offside stands.

High School exam: You would likely not call this an offense. High school rules often have a more liberal interpretation. In this case, since the player was in an offside position when the ball was played and they made no attempt to get involved, their position is reset by their lack of involvement. When they later received the ball from the defender and scored, it would be considered a legitimate goal.

So, the key difference is that college officiating is stricter, requiring any involvement after an initial offside offense to be penalized, while high school rules might be more lenient, especially if the player shows no intention to get involved.
