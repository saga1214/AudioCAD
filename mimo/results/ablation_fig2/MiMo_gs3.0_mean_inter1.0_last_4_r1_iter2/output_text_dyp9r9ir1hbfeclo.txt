That's a great question to clarify! While you correctly identified the three primary **bones** for rotation—the **humerus** (upper arm), **scapula** (shoulder blade), and **clavicle** (collarbone)—the question of a "fourth component" might be referencing the **soft tissues** that connect them and enable smooth movement.

Here's the breakdown:

1.  **The Three Bones:**
    *   **Humerus:** Upper arm bone, head fits into the scapula's socket.
    *   **Scapula:** Shoulder blade, provides the shallow socket (glenoid fossa) for the humeral head.
    *   **Clavicle:** Collarbone, connects the arm (humerus) to the main frame of the body (scapula/sternum), allowing arm movement relative to the torso.

2.  **The Fourth Key Component (The "Fourth" You're Likely Thinking Of):**
    *   **Rotator Cuff Muscles:** While not a bone, the **rotator cuff** is absolutely essential for smooth rotation and stability. It's the group of four muscles/tendons that dynamically hold the humeral head snugly within the shallow socket of the scapula, preventing it from slipping out while allowing rotation. They are:
        *   **Supraspinatus:** Initiates upward rotation (abduction).
        *   **Infraspinatus:** External rotation (turning arm out).
        *   **Teres Minor:** External rotation.
        *   **Subscapularis:** Internal rotation (turning arm in).
    *   **Ligaments:** The shoulder also has key ligaments (like the glenohumeral ligaments) that reinforce the joint capsule and limit excessive movement.

**In Summary:**

*   **Bones working for rotation:** Humerus, Scapula, Clavicle.
*   **The Fourth Crucial Component for Rotation:** The **Rotator Cuff Muscles** (Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres Minor, Subscapularis) and supporting ligaments work *with* those bones to provide the stability and control needed for smooth rotation.

Regarding your friend's soreness from lateral raises: Yes, it could potentially be a rotator cuff issue, but it's also very common for the **deltoid muscle** (especially the anterior fiber) to feel sore during this exercise. Rotator cuff pain often feels more deep, achy, or catching, while deltoid soreness is usually more superficial and muscle-burning. If the pain is persistent, sharp, or accompanied by weakness, it's definitely worth getting evaluated.
