For a modern-day art museum, the **first and foundational line of defense is always controlling physical and digital access at every entry point.** This is the most critical layer because it establishes the baseline for managing exactly who and what enters both public areas and restricted zones (like storage, offices, and server rooms).

Here’s how it works in modern contexts:
1. **Public Entry Points:** Visible security personnel combined with screening procedures (e.g., X-ray bag checks, prohibited items lists) deter threats and manage entry. This is your first human interface with potential intruders.
2. **Staff Access:** Secure entry systems like biometric scanners, multi-factor authentication, or encrypted keycard systems restrict access to non-public areas. This minimizes human error by automating verification.
3. **Perimeter Perimeters:** Modern extensions include automated barriers, vehicle detection, and proximity sensors around the building to trigger alerts if unauthorized approaches occur before anyone even reaches the doors.

**Why this is non-negotiable:**  
The Isabella Stewart Gardner heist (1990) was enabled by human error at access points – guards who *should* have followed protocol by not letting police officers inside after hours. This case underscores that the first line of defense must be **redundant and automated** where possible to mitigate human fallibility. Modern systems layer this access control with surveillance, alarms, and secure displays, but **entry control remains the primary choke point** – it’s where threats are stopped before they can exploit vulnerabilities deeper inside the museum.
