You've hit on a crucial nuance! While the Portuguese voyages *were* underway before 1453, the fall of Constantinople acted as a **massive accelerator and catalyst** rather than the sole starting point. Here's a more nuanced breakdown:

1.  **The Pre-1453 Context:** You're absolutely right. Portuguese explorers like Henry the Navigator were already systematically exploring the African coast (e.g., Prince Henry's School of Navigation, voyages to the Canaries, Cape Bojador). The desire for a sea route to Asia was a *long-term* ambition, not a sudden event triggered solely by 1453.

2.  **Constantinople's Role (Gradual Disruption):** Trade *wasn't* instantly closed in 1453. The Venetians and Genoese still controlled key ports like Alexandria and Cairo for years. However:
    *   **Ottoman Control:** After 1453, Ottoman power grew, making trade increasingly risky and costly for Western Europeans.
    *   **Price Inflation:** The Ottomans began imposing higher taxes and duties on goods passing through their territories.
    *   **Geopolitical Threat:** The capture of the Byzantine capital symbolized the decline of the old overland routes and the rise of a powerful Islamic rival controlling the eastern Mediterranean.

3.  **The Catalyst Effect (1453 Onwards):**
    *   **Symbolic Shock:** The fall of Constantinople was a profound psychological blow to Christendom. It galvanized European rulers and merchants, making the search for alternative routes to spread Christianity feel more urgent.
    *   **Strategic Competition:** Powerful kingdoms (Spain, Portugal, England, France, Italy) saw the event as proof that their rivals *could* be challenged. This intensified competition for wealth, glory, and trade dominance. Finding a new route became a national imperative.
    *   **Focus & Funding:** The disruption made the *need* for an independent sea route undeniable. Rulers like Castile and Aragon (funding Columbus) and Portugal's King Manuel I (funding da Gama) dramatically increased investment in exploration *specifically* because the old routes were now clearly threatened or controlled.
    *   **Multiplier Effect:** The event amplified *all* the pre-existing drivers: technological ambition, economic desire, religious zeal, and competitive rivalry. It gave them a shared, urgent goal.

**Conclusion:**

*   **No, Constantinople wasn't the *only* cause.** The technological advances in shipbuilding (caravel) and navigation (magnetic compass, astrolabe) were prerequisites. The desire for trade and competition existed before 1453.
*   **Yes, it was a critical *catalyst* and *accelerator*.** The fall of Constantinople transformed a long-term aspiration into an urgent, continent-wide imperative. It:
    *   **Dramatically increased the perceived necessity** of finding a sea route to Asia.
    *   **Galvanized religious and national competition** among European powers.
    *   **Provided a powerful symbolic and strategic motivation** for rulers to fund large-scale voyages.
    *   **Created a sense of shared crisis** that unified European efforts (in a way) towards exploration.

Portuguese voyages were already underway, but the fall of Constantinople provided the crucial "why now?" and the massive influx of resources and political will that propelled the era into full-scale, continent-wide activity. It turned a promising start into a desperate race for survival and advantage.
