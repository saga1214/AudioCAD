You're very close! The word is "saccadic" (pronounced suh-KAH-dik), which refers to the movement itself. "Saccadic masking" is the phenomenon where the brain suppresses visual input during these eye movements.

So, to answer your question: No, it wouldn’t be correct to say the eye sees continuously. Instead, the eye and brain work together in a very dynamic way.

The eye itself doesn’t perceive motion as a continuous stream of data. It detects light and movement in small, rapidly changing patches. When your eye moves, like during a saccade, it briefly blocks incoming visual information to avoid sensory overload and motion blur. This is saccadic masking.

The brain, however, takes this incomplete or fragmented information and reconstructs a smooth, continuous perception of movement. It fills in the gaps and blends the images from the moments in between saccades, creating the illusion of continuous motion.

So, to put it simply: The eye senses movement in discrete, rapidly updating snapshots, while the brain processes and synthesizes these snapshots into the continuous, fluid motion we experience in real life. 

Think of it like this: The eye is the sensor, and the brain is the editor. The eye captures fast, jerky data, but the brain makes it all look smooth.
