Well, I suppose there are a few ways to think about it. On one hand, you could say that the most important aspect is the sheer irreplaceable uniqueness of the species. The fact that they are found nowhere else on Earth and that their loss is, you know, a permanent loss of genetic information and potential knowledge. That's a huge part of it.

On the other hand, I suppose you could argue that the most critical aspect is the practical, tangible value to humanity. That is, the ecosystem services they provide—things like clean water, pollination, and carbon storage. This is especially true for the 35% of people who depend directly on them for their survival. That's a very concrete reason to protect them.

So, I guess it really depends on whether you define "most important" as the intrinsic value to nature itself, or the instrumental value to human survival and well-being. Both are, I suppose, incredibly important.
