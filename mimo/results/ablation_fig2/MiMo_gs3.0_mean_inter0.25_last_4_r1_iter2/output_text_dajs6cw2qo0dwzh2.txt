You're absolutely right to challenge that oversimplification. **Constantinople's fall wasn't the sole spark, nor was it the *immediate* trigger for Portuguese exploration.** Your points highlight crucial nuances:

1.  **Pre-1453 Exploration:** As you noted, Portuguese voyages down the African coast (like Henry the Navigator's explorations) began *before* 1453 and continued *afterward*. They were actively creating a viable alternative route.
2.  **Constantinople's Role:** Its fall wasn't about instantly *closing* trade. It was about creating a **long-term, existential threat** to the existing spice trade routes controlled by Italian city-states (like Venice) and Muslim powers. The Ottomans were consolidating power, increasing taxes, and potentially tightening control over land routes – making trade increasingly risky and expensive.
3.  **The Catalyst Effect:** While trade wasn't instantly cut off, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was a massive psychological and political shock:
    *   **Reinforced Motivation:** It proved the Ottoman threat was real and growing, accelerating the urgency for Europeans to find independent sea routes to Asia.
    *   **Competitive Pressure:** Powerful kingdoms (Spain, Portugal, England, France, Italy) competed fiercely for wealth, glory, and spreading Christianity. Constantinople's fall intensified this competition for a first-mover advantage in finding new routes.
    *   **Symbolic Event:** It shattered the medieval world order centered on Byzantium and Islamic powers controlling the eastward flow of goods and knowledge.

**So, what led? It was a *combination* of factors, with Constantinople's fall acting as a powerful *accelerant* and *symbolic catalyst*:**

*   **Search for Direct Trade:** Primary driver: Access to Asian spices, silks, and other goods without paying hefty middlemen (Ottomans, Italians, Muslims).
*   **Technological Advancements:** Critical enablers:
    *   **Shipbuilding:** Caravels (Portuguese), lateen sails, improved hull designs for long-distance ocean voyages.
    *   **Navigation:** Improved astrolabe/quadrant for latitude, portolan charts, better understanding of winds/currents, magnetic compass refinement.
*   **Rise of European Nation-States:** Powerful monarchies (like Spain/Portugal) with resources, ambitions, and a desire for global prestige and wealth, willing to fund expensive expeditions.
*   **Religious Zeal:** Desire to spread Christianity, particularly among Portugal and Spain.
*   **The Ottoman Threat (Constantinople as Symbol):** While trade wasn't instantly closed, the fall in 1453 was a stark warning that the old routes were vulnerable and controlled by a rising power hostile to European interests. This significantly **increased the perceived necessity** for finding new sea routes *soon*.
*   **Portuguese Pre-Emption:** Portugal was already leading the way, exploring the African coast and developing the necessary technology and knowledge. The fall of Constantinople provided the *political and economic impetus* for them (and others) to press forward aggressively.

**In essence:** The Age of Discovery was driven by the **search for wealth, enabled by technology, fueled by competition and ambition, and significantly accelerated by the perceived threat posed by the Ottoman Empire after Constantinople fell.** Constantinople wasn't the *only* cause, but it was a major catalyst that made the quest for new sea routes seem not just desirable, but urgent and necessary for European powers.
