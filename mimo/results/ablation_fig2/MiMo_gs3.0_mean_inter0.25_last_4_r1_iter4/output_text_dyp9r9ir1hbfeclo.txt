You've picked up on a key distinction! Here's the clarification:

1.  **Collaborating *Bones* for Rotation:** The three bones collaborating directly for shoulder rotation are:
    *   **Humerus:** Upper arm bone.
    *   **Scapula:** Shoulder blade (holds the shallow socket).
    *   **Clavicle:** Collarbone (connects the scapula to the sternum and provides stability).

    These three *bones* form the bony framework for the shoulder joint and its rotation.

2.  **The Fourth Component (Muscles):** The fourth structure critically involved in shoulder rotation is part of the **rotator cuff**:
    *   **Teres Minor:** This is the fourth muscle/tendon in the rotator cuff group.

**Why the Confusion?**

*   **Clavicle:** While not part of the *glenohumeral* joint itself (the main shoulder joint), the clavicle is the fourth *bone* essential for the overall shoulder complex and its stability. It connects the scapula to the axial skeleton and is crucial for arm elevation and rotation.
*   **Rotator Cuff Muscles:** The rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) is a group of *muscles and tendons*, not bones. They are absolutely vital for stabilizing the humeral head in the scapula's socket during rotation and movement, but they are not bones.

**In Summary:**

*   For **bone collaboration and basic joint formation**, it's the **Humerus, Scapula, and Clavicle**.
*   For **critical muscular stabilization and support during rotation**, it's the **Rotator Cuff**, with the **Teres Minor** being the fourth specific muscle component.

Your friend's soreness during lateral raises *could* be related to the rotator cuff (especially if it's sharp or localized pain), but it could also be normal muscle fatigue or strain from the deltoids. If the pain is persistent or worsening, medical evaluation is important.
