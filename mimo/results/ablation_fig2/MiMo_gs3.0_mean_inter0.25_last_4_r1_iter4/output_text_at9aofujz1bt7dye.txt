Yeah, that’s a great way to put it! At rink temperatures of minus ten degrees Celsius, the coefficient of friction can dip to as low as 0.005 to 0.008. This is because the ice surface becomes harder, which reduces the formation of that thin layer of liquid water that helps with gliding. Skaters can push off with more force without too much drag, which is why they can achieve those powerful jumps and spins. But it’s a delicate balance, because if it gets too cold, say minus fifteen degrees or colder, friction might start to rise again as the ice gets even harder and the blade doesn’t cut as cleanly.
