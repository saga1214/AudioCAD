Yes, you're right to question that. The eye doesn't see continuously in the way a camera does. It's more like a series of very rapid, still images. Saccadic masking is a key part of this. 

Let me break it down. The word is pronounced "sah-kah-dik." The 'c' is pronounced like a 'k', and the 'i' is like 'ee' in 'see'.

Now, back to the main point. Your eye is always moving—either slowly to track something or in those quick saccades to shift focus. During a saccade, your brain basically shuts off the visual processing to prevent motion sickness and other issues. 

This means that even though your eye is constantly moving, your brain is stitching together the visual information it receives into a continuous experience. It’s a bit like a camera that takes a few still photos per second and then blends them together to create the illusion of smooth motion. 

So, to answer your question: No, the eye doesn't see continuously in the sense of a steady stream of information. It's more like a series of snapshots that the brain combines to create the perception of continuous motion. 

The saccadic masking phenomenon is a big reason for this. It explains why we don't perceive the world as a series of disjointed frames but rather as a seamless, flowing experience.
