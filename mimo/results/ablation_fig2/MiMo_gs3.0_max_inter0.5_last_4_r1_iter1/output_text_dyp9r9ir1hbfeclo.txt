That's a great question to clarify! While the **three main bones** (humerus, scapula, clavicle) form the *structural framework* for the shoulder, the fourth critical component that enables rotation is the **rotator cuff muscles**.  

So, to break it down:  
1.  **Bones Working Together:**  
    *   **Humerus:** Upper arm bone (head fits into the socket).  
    *   **Scapula:** Shoulder blade (shallow socket holds the humeral head).  
    *   Rotation primarily occurs at the **glenohumeral joint** (where the humerus meets the scapula).  
    *   **Clavicle Collarbone:** Stabilizes the scapula and connects the arm to the axial skeleton, indirectly supporting shoulder movement.  

2.  **Enabling Rotation (The "Fourth Component"):**  
    *   The **rotator cuff muscles** (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) are the key players.  
    *   They surround the shoulder joint and work in concert to:  
        *   **Stabilize** the humeral head in the shallow socket of the scapula.  
        *   **Control** the movement of the humerus within the scapula.  
        *   **Provide the muscle power** for internal and external rotation.  
    *   Without the rotator cuff, the humeral head would dislocate easily during movement.  

**In Summary:**  
The **bones** (humerus, scapula, clavicle) create the anatomical structure. The **rotator cuff muscles** provide the active force and stability necessary for smooth rotation. Your friend's soreness from lateral raises *could* be rotator cuff-related if it's sharp, localized, or worsens with overhead motion – but mild soreness is often just normal muscle fatigue.
