For modern-day art museum security, the **first line of defense remains controlling access at every public and restricted entry point**, but it must be designed to mitigate human error—a critical vulnerability highlighted by heists like the 1990 Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum robbery.  

### Why this is foundational:  
1. **Prevents Unauthorized Entry**: Screening procedures (e.g., bag checks, pat-downs) and visible security personnel manage exactly *who* and *what* enters the museum, blocking threats before they escalate.  
2. **Deters Crime**: A high, consistent security presence discourages potential attackers, addressing human impulsivity or opportunism.  
3. **Protocols Over Reliance on Humans**: After the Gardner heist (where guards broke protocol by letting imposters in), museums now emphasize strict access rules—like keycard-locked doors for staff areas—to reduce human error.  

### Key Implementation:  
- **Visible Guards + Screening**: Mandatory for all visitor entrances.  
- **Bag Policies**: Prohibit or restrict large bags (common in thefts).  
- **Automated Barriers**: Turnstiles or scanners with keycard access for staff.  

While high-tech layers (e.g., cameras, alarms) follow *after* entry, the first line acts as the **primary chokepoint** to stop threats before they compromise the museum. Human error remains a persistent risk, making robust, protocol-driven access control essential—not just a technical layer, but a behavioral one.
