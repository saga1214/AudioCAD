Yeah, that’s a good way to think about it. At rink temperatures around minus ten degrees Celsius, the coefficient of friction is super low, but not quite as low as 0.005. It’s probably closer to the 0.01 to 0.02 range, like we’ve been talking about. If it gets colder, say below minus fifteen degrees, friction might increase slightly, maybe going up to around 0.02 or even 0.03, because that water layer becomes even less likely to form. But it’s not going to drop to 0.005 or 0.008 just because of the temperature. The blade sharpness and pressure are still the main factors. So, at minus ten degrees, it’s definitely in that super low range, just not in the sub-zero point-oh-one territory.
