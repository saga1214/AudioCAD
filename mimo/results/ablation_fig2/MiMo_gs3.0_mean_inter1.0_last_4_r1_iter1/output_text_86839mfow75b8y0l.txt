You're absolutely right to focus on that – it's a crucial distinction! While **Quincy Jones** is the most direct answer for a songwriter *whose work inherently demonstrates* how production shapes emotion and dynamics, **Sia, Gamble & Huff, and Max Martin** also bring significant production sensibility to their songwriting, though it might be less overtly obvious in every single hit they wrote for others.

Here's a breakdown of why Quincy Jones is the prime example, and how the others blend songwriting and production:

1.  **Quincy Jones (The Master of Production as Emotion & Dynamics):**
    *   **Why he fits perfectly:** Jones was (and is) a visionary producer whose genius lies in *orchestrating the entire sonic landscape* to serve the song's emotional core and create dynamic shifts. He understood that the *way* a song sounds is inseparable from *what* the song is saying.
    *   **How Production Enhances:**
        *   **Emotion:** His use of orchestration (strings, horns, choirs), specific instruments, vocal layering, and sonic textures (like the warmth of analog tape) was incredibly evocative. Think of the lush strings in Michael Jackson's "Man in the Mirror" amplifying the yearning, or the stark minimalism in "Thriller" creating pure tension.
        *   **Dynamics:** Jones was a master of building and releasing tension through arrangement and production. Tracks like "Billie Jean" or "Beat It" have incredible dynamic range – quiet verses build to explosive choruses and guitar solos, crafted *sonically*. The production choices *create* the dynamic journey.
        *   **Storytelling:** The production became part of the narrative. The shift from the sparse intro to the full band on "Off the Wall" wasn't just a change in volume; it was a sonic metaphor for emotional awakening.
    *   **Studying Him:** Listen to the *albums* he produced (Michael Jackson, Stevie Wonder, Frank Sinatra, Diana Ross, Earth Wind & Fire). Pay attention to how the *sound* makes you *feel* and how the arrangement builds towards moments of impact. Understand how silence, space, and texture are used as tools.

2.  **Bruce Swedien (The Sonic Architect):**
    *   **Why he's worth studying:** While primarily a legendary audio engineer/producer, Swedien worked *incredibly closely* with Quincy Jones and Michael Jackson. His work is inseparable from the emotional impact of those records.
    *   **How Production Enhances:** Swedien's engineering captured the warmth, intimacy, and clarity that became the signature sound. His ability to make voices and instruments feel present, rich, and emotionally resonant was key to the "human" feel of songs like "Billie Jean" or "She's Out of My Life." The production choices (mic placement, room sound, mixing) *were* the emotional foundation.
    *   **Studying Him:** Focus on the *sound quality* and *clarity* in his work. Notice how the vocals cut through, how the instruments breathe, and how the overall mix feels intimate yet powerful. How does the sonic texture contribute to the song's mood?

3.  **Max Martin & Gamble & Huff (Melody & Soul Meets Modern/Classic Production):**
    *   **Max Martin:** His genius is in crafting the *perfect pop hook* through melody, chord progressions, and vocal delivery. However, his production is meticulously crafted *around* that hook to maximize its impact. He understands how compression, EQ, vocal doubling, and synth sounds *make the chorus hit harder* and the pre-chorus build tension. The production *serves and amplifies* the songwriting core.
    *   **Gamble & Huff:** They were masters of creating infectious, danceable *grooves* and soulful, relatable *melodies* in the 70s. Their production was rooted in the R&B/funk sound of the time but infused it with a pop sensibility. The tightness of the rhythm section, the warmth of the horns, the soulful backing vocals – these production choices *created* the dynamic feel and emotional warmth of Philly Soul hits.
    *   **Studying Them:** Analyze *why* the chorus feels so massive. What production techniques (layering, effects, mixing balance) make the hook *stick* in your head? How does the rhythm section create the danceable feel? How do the backing vocals add emotional depth? The production choices are integral to making the songwriting *feel* the way it does.

**In Summary:**

*   **For pure songwriting structure/hook craft (Pop Modern):** Max Martin, Sia, Taylor Swift, Ryan Tedder.
*   **For soulful, groove-driven pop/R&B songwriting:** Gamble & Huff, Taylor Swift (earlier work), Bruno Mars.
*   **For understanding how production *itself* is a primary tool for emotion and dynamics (especially in classic pop/R&B):** **Quincy Jones** is the undisputed master. Bruce Swedien is essential to understanding the sonic foundation of Jones's work with MJ.
*   **For seeing how production *serves and amplifies* the pop hook:** Max Martin, modern producers working with artists like Ed Sheeran or Ariana Grande.

**Actionable Advice:** Listen to Quincy Jones' albums *with headphones* and actively listen for the *sound* – the textures, the spaces, the way instruments enter and exit, the vocal processing. Notice how these choices make you *feel* and how they build towards or release tension. You'll hear songwriting made tangible through production. Good luck!
