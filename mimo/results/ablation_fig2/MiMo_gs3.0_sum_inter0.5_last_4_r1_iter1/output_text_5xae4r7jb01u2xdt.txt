Got it. Let's look at a special exception using college and high school rules side-by-side.

First, the college rule. Imagine this situation: The Red Team is attacking the Blue Team's goal. The Red forward, number nine, is in an offside position when their teammate, number ten, plays a back-pass to the second-to-last defender. The ball then deflects off that defender's foot before continuing to the number nine player, who controls it and scores.

In college soccer, this deflecting rebound does reset the situation. So, the offside offense does not apply.

Now, for the high school rule, let's use a very similar situation. This time, the Red forward, number nine, is in an offside position when their teammate, number ten, plays a pass forward directly to the second-to-last defender. That defender makes what the referee would see as a deliberate, controlled touch on the ball before the ball continues to the number nine player, who then scores.

Under high school rules, this defender's deliberate play resets the offside situation. So, the high school referee would also call no offside offense in this case.

So, what's the key difference you asked about? In this specific example of a defender's touch, the rules are actually identical between college and high school. The confusion often comes from other restart exceptions, like whether being received directly from a goal kick throws the attacker on or offside. But for a defender's touch resetting an offside call, they are the same.
