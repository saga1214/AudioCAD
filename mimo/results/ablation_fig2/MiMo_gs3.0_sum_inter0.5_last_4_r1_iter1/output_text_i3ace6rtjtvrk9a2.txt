That's an excellent question—and you're absolutely right to separate "what the eye can see" from "what the brain processes." Let's break this down clearly, using scientific terminology:

### 1. **Saccadic Masking (Pronunciation: "suh-KAH-dik")**
   - **What it is**: A neural mechanism where the brain suppresses visual processing during rapid eye movements (saccades).  
   - **Why it happens**: The brain prioritizes stability over motion detection during saccades to prevent motion blur and cognitive overload.  
   - **Duration**: The "blind spot" during saccades lasts ~100–200 milliseconds, depending on speed.  

### 2. **Eye vs. Brain Perception**
   - **Eye (Optical Sensor)**:  
     - Detects light changes as discrete packets (photons).  
     - Photoreceptors (rods/cones) respond to *changes* in light, not static images.  
     - **Critical Fact**: The eye does **not** capture a continuous "stream" of images. It samples visual information at irregular intervals during saccades.  
   - **Brain (Visual Cortex)**:  
     - Processes *changes* in visual input (e.g., motion, contrast).  
     - Fills gaps between saccadic samples using predictive modeling (e.g., "filling in" the blind spot).  
     - Creates the illusion of continuous vision via **temporal interpolation**.  

### 3. **Perceptual Limits**
   - **最低感知阈值**:  
     - **16–20 fps**: Movement starts appearing smooth (minimum for natural motion).  
     - **60 fps**: Feels like real-time motion (broadcast video standard).  
     - **120 fps**: Motion becomes hyper-real (e.g., sports, CGI).  
   - **上限 (Upper Limit)**:  
     - Humans can perceive motion changes up to **~100–120 fps** in ideal conditions (studies by Trovich, 1950s).  
     - Beyond this, differences become imperceptible for most people.  

### 4. **Key Distinction**
   - **Eye**: Detects discrete, time-gated samples of light.  
   - **Brain**: Constructs a *perceptual* continuous experience from these samples.  
   - **Implication for Filmmaking**:  
     - At **24 fps**, the brain "fills" gaps so effectively that movement feels natural despite eye sampling gaps.  
     - At **60 fps**, the brain has more data to interpolate, reducing "judder" in fast motion.  

### Practical Takeaway for Filmmaking
- **35mm at 24 fps**: Feels "dreamy" because the brain’s interpolation creates a stylized, non-literal experience.  
- **Video at 30/25 fps**: Closer to real-time motion, but still relies on the same perceptual mechanisms.  
- **High fps (120+)**: Useful for slow-motion, but requires careful editing to avoid hyper-realism.  

In short: **The eye sees in fragments; the brain sees continuously.** Your question highlights the critical difference between biological perception and engineered media systems. As a filmmaker, understanding this will deepen your creative choices!
