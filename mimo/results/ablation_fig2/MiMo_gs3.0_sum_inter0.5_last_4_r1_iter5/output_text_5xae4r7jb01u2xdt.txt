The main difference in offside for college soccer is between the Laws of the Game from the International Football Association Board, or IFAB, and the Laws of the Game from the Laws of the Game Committee in the United States.

Let me give you an example to show the difference. Imagine this situation: The red team, let's call them Red A, is attacking. Their forward, number nine, is in an offside position when a teammate passes the ball forward. The ball takes a big bounce off a Red A defender and deflects forward. Blue team forward number seven then gets to the ball first and kicks it to their teammate, who scores.

Now, here's how the two rulebooks interpret that defender's touch differently.

According to the IFAB rules, which are used internationally and in many college games, a deliberate controlled play resets offside. Since the ball simply deflected or rebounded off the defender, the original offside offense still applies, and the goal is disallowed.

However, under the U.S. Laws of the Game, the goal would be allowed. In the U.S. rules, a defender's touch resets offside even if it isn't a 'deliberate and controlled play.' Any contact, like a bounce or rebound, is considered a reset.

So, to summarize: In the example you gave, Red A scores. Under IFAB rules, it's an offside offense because the ball just bounced off the defender. But under U.S. rules, the situation is reset by the defender's touch, and it's a legal goal. Referees studying for your college certification should focus on IFAB principles, but since you also referee high school games, being familiar with the U.S. Law of the Game is also important.
