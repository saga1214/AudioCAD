That's a really thoughtful question, and you're touching on a key debate among historians.

The short answer is: **no, the Age of Discovery wouldn't have happened *exactly* the same way or at the same time without the fall of Constantinople.**

Here's why:

1. **Motivation and urgency**: The Portuguese were exploring the African coast in the early 1400s under Prince Henry the Navigator, but their efforts were more incremental. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 acted as a powerful catalyst. It made the search for a sea route to Asia not just an economic opportunity, but a strategic necessity for survival and competitiveness.

2. **Funding and royal support**: The urgency created by the loss of Constantinople helped monarchs like King John II of Portugal justify massive investments in exploration. Without that crisis, it might have taken much longer for such funding to materialize.

3. **Broader European involvement**: The Ottoman threat didn't just affect Portugal. It pushed Spain and other powers to invest in exploration too. Columbus's 1492 voyage, for example, was partly motivated by the desire to bypass Ottoman-controlled routes.

So while the *technical* groundwork was already being laid by the Portuguese, the fall of Constantinople dramatically accelerated the pace and scale of exploration, turning what might have been a slow, regional effort into a full-blown "Age of Discovery" across Europe.

In short: **The timing and scale were likely different without that event.**
