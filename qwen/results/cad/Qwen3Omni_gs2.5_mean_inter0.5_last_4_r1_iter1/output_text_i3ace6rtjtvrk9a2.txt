Thank you for the correction — you're absolutely right to ask! The word is **saccade**, pronounced:

**/ˈsæk.əd/** (SAK-uhd)

It comes from the French word *saccade*, meaning a sudden jerky movement — which is exactly what it is in vision.

Now, about your question: **"Would it be correct to say that the eye sees continuously?"**

Yes and no — it depends on what you mean by "sees."

Let’s clarify:

- **The eye does not see continuously** in the way a camera might. Instead, it takes in visual information in rapid snapshots — about 10–12 times per second — and sends those signals to the brain. These are called **fixations** (when the eye is still and focused) and **saccades** (the quick jumps between fixations).

- During a saccade, the brain **suppresses visual input** — this is **saccadic masking** — so you don’t perceive the blur or motion of your own eye moving. That’s why you don’t see the world “jumping” when you move your eyes.

- Between saccades, during fixations, the eye captures relatively stable visual information. The brain then **stitches together** these snapshots into a smooth, continuous perception of the world.

So, while the **eye** is not capturing a continuous image, the **brain** creates the illusion of continuity by filling in gaps and smoothing transitions.

Therefore, it’s more accurate to say:

👉 **The brain perceives the world as continuous**, even though the eye captures it in discrete snapshots.

This is why movies work: they show 24 still images per second, and your brain blends them into smooth motion.

So, to answer your original question: **No, the eye does not see continuously.** But **yes, the brain creates the perception of continuous vision.**
