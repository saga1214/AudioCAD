### 1. Pronunciation of "saccadic masking"

The correct word is **saccadic**, pronounced:

**/səˈkæd.ɪk/**  
(“suh-KAD-ik”)

Breakdown:
- **suh** — like the "a" in "sofa"
- **KAD** — like "cad" as in "cadence"
- **ik** — like "ic" in "magic"

So: **suh-KAD-ik**

And **saccadic masking** is the full term.

It refers to the phenomenon where your brain suppresses visual input during rapid eye movements (saccades), so you don’t perceive the blur or motion.

---

### 2. Does the eye "see continuously"?

This is a really important distinction.

**No, the eye does not see continuously.**

Here’s why:

- Your eyes are constantly making tiny, rapid movements called **microsaccades** and larger ones called **saccades**.
- During each saccade, your brain **temporarily suppresses visual input** — this is saccadic masking.
- So, you’re not seeing a continuous stream of images. Instead, your brain stitches together snapshots from the moments between saccades.

Think of it like this:  
Imagine you’re watching a movie, but the projector flickers on and off every 1/20th of a second. But because your brain fills in the gaps, you don’t notice the flicker — you see a smooth image.

Similarly, your brain takes the visual snapshots from the brief moments when your eyes are still, and fills in the motion between them. That’s how you perceive **continuous motion** — even though your eyes are actually capturing discrete moments.

So, to answer your question:

> **"Would it be correct to say that the eye sees continuously?"**

**No — it would not be correct.**

The eye captures visual information in discrete snapshots (like a camera), but the brain processes and integrates that information to create the **illusion of continuous vision**.

That’s why we perceive the world as smooth and stable, even though our eyes are constantly jumping around and our brain is doing a lot of "filling in" behind the scenes.
