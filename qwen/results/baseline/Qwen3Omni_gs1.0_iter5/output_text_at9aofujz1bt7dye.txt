Hmm, actually, even though the friction increases a bit at colder temperatures, it's not quite that low. At minus ten degrees Celsius, the coefficient of friction on ice is typically still in the range of about 0.01 to 0.02—maybe slightly higher than at the optimal minus five degrees. So while it's still very low, it's not going down to 0.005 or 0.008. In fact, those values are more typical of the *best* conditions on a well-maintained rink at around minus five degrees, where the blade pressure melts a thin layer of water, creating that super slick surface. So colder ice doesn't make it *more* slippery—it can actually make it *less* slippery, which is why rinks are kept at that sweet spot, not too cold.
